# 6.2 Orthogonal complements and the matrix transpose

We've now seen how the dot product enables us to determine the angle between two vectors and, more specifically, when two vectors are orthogonal. Moving forward, we will explore how the orthogonality condition simplifies many common tasks, such as expressing a vector as a linear combination of a given set of vectors.

This section introduces the notion of an orthogonal complement, the set of vectors each of which is orthogonal to a prescribed subspace. We'll also find a way to describe dot products using matrix products, which allows us to study orthogonality using many of the tools for understanding linear systems that we developed earlier.

Given a subspace $W$ of $\real^m$, the **orthogonal complement** of $W$ is the set of vectors in $\real^m$ each of which is orthogonal to every vector in $W$. We denote the orthogonal complement by $W^\perp$.

The previous activity demonstrates an important connection between the matrix transpose and dot products. More specifically, the components of the product $A^T\xvec$ are simply the dot products of the columns of $A$ with $\xvec$. We will make frequent use of this observation so let's record it as a proposition.

<a id="prop-transpose-multiplication" class="ula-anchor"></a>

:::::: proposition 命题 6.2.1

If $A$ is the matrix whose columns are $\vvec_1,\vvec_2,\ldots,\vvec_n$, then 

$$
A^T\xvec =
\begin{bmatrix}
\vvec_1\cdot\xvec \\
\vvec_2\cdot\xvec \\
\vdots \\
\vvec_n\cdot\xvec \\
\end{bmatrix}
$$

::::::

<a id="6-2-example-4" class="ula-anchor"></a>

:::::: example 例 6.2.4

Suppose that $W$ is a subspace of $\real^4$ having basis 

$$
\begin{aligned}
\wvec_1=\fourvec1021,\hspace{24pt}
\wvec_2=\fourvec2134\text{,}
\end{aligned}
$$

 and that we wish to describe the orthogonal complement $W^\perp$.

If $A$ is the matrix 

$$
A = \begin{bmatrix}\wvec_1 &
\wvec_2\end{bmatrix}
$$

 and $\xvec$ is in $W^\perp$, we have 

$$
\begin{aligned}
A^T\xvec = \twovec{\wvec_1\cdot\xvec}{\wvec_2\cdot\xvec} =
\twovec00\text{.}
\end{aligned}
$$

 Describing vectors $\xvec$ that are orthogonal to both $\wvec_1$ and $\wvec_2$ is therefore equivalent to the more familiar task of describing the solution set $A^T\xvec = \zerovec$. To do so, we find the reduced row echelon form of $A^T$ and write the solution set parametrically as 

$$
\begin{aligned}
\xvec = x_3\fourvec{-2}{1}10 +
x_4\fourvec{-1}{-2}01\text{.}
\end{aligned}
$$

 Once again, the distributive property of dot products tells us that such a vector is also orthogonal to any linear combination of $\wvec_1$ and $\wvec_2$ so this solution set is, in fact, the orthogonal complement $W^\perp$. Indeed, we see that the vectors 

$$
\begin{aligned}
\vvec_1=\fourvec{-2}110,\hspace{24pt}
\vvec_2=\fourvec{-1}{-2}01
\end{aligned}
$$

 form a basis for $W^\perp$, which is a two-dimensional subspace of $\real^4$.

::::::

To place this example in a slightly more general context, note that $\wvec_1$ and $\wvec_2$, the columns of $A$, form a basis of $W$. Since $\col(A)$, the column space of $A$ is the subspace of linear combinations of the columns of $A$, we have $W=\col(A)$.

This example also shows that the orthogonal complement $W^\perp = \col(A)^\perp$ is described by the solution set of $A^T\xvec = \zerovec$. This solution set is what we have called $\nul(A^T)$, the null space of $A^T$. In this way, we see the following proposition, which is visually represented in [图 6.2.2](/chapter-6/6-2#fig-orthog-comp).

<a id="prop-col-orthog" class="ula-anchor"></a>

:::::: proposition 命题 6.2.2

For any matrix $A$, the orthogonal complement of $\col(A)$ is $\nul(A^T)$; that is, 

$$
\begin{aligned}
\col(A)^\perp = \nul(A^T)\text{.}
\end{aligned}
$$

<a id="assemblage-prop-transpose" class="ula-anchor"></a>

:::::: note Properties of the transpose

Here are some properties of the matrix transpose, expressed in terms of general matrices $A$, $B$, and $C$. We assume that $C$ is a square matrix. 

- If $A+B$ is defined, then $(A+B)^T =
 A^T+B^T$.
- $(sA)^T = sA^T$.
- $(A^T)^T = A$.
- $\det(C) = \det(C^T)$.
- If $AB$ is defined, then $(AB)^T = B^TA^T$. Notice that the order of the multiplication is reversed.
- If $C$ is invertible, then $(C^T)^{-1} =
 (C^{-1})^T$.

There is one final property we wish to record though we will wait until 第 7.4 节 to explain why it is true.

<a id="prop-col-row-rank" class="ula-anchor"></a>

:::::: proposition 命题 6.2.3

For any matrix $A$, we have 

$$
\begin{aligned}
\rank(A) = \rank(A^T)\text{.}
\end{aligned}
$$

::::::

This proposition is important because it implies a relationship between the dimensions of a subspace and its orthogonal complement. For instance, if $A$ is an $m\times n$ matrix, we saw in 第 3.5 节 that $\dim\col(A) = \rank(A)$ and $\dim\nul(A) =
n-\rank(A)$.

Now suppose that $W$ is an $n$-dimensional subspace of $\real^m$ with basis $\wvec_1,\wvec_2,\ldots,\wvec_n$. If we form the $m\times n$ matrix 

$$
A=\begin{bmatrix}\wvec_1 & \wvec_2
& \ldots & \wvec_n\end{bmatrix}
$$

, then $\col(A) = W$ so that 

$$
\begin{aligned}
\rank(A) = \dim\col(A) = \dim W = n\text{.}
\end{aligned}
$$

The transpose $A^T$ is an $n\times m$ matrix having $\rank(A^T) = \rank(A)= n$. Since $W^\perp =
\nul(A^T)$, we have 

$$
\begin{aligned}
\dim W^\perp = \dim\nul(A^T) = m - \rank(A^T) = m-n =
m-\dim W\text{.}
\end{aligned}
$$

 This explains the following proposition.

<a id="prop-orthog-dim" class="ula-anchor"></a>

:::::: proposition 命题 6.2.4

If $W$ is a subspace of $\real^m$, then 

$$
\begin{aligned}
\dim W + \dim W^\perp = m\text{.}
\end{aligned}
$$

This section introduced the matrix transpose, its connection to dot products, and its use in describing the orthogonal complement of a subspace. 

- The columns of the matrix $A$ are the rows of the matrix transpose $A^T$.
- The components of the product $A^T\xvec$ are the dot products of $\xvec$ with the columns of $A$.
- The orthogonal complement of the column space of $A$ equals the null space of $A^T$; that is, $\col(A)^\perp = \nul(A^T)$.
- If $W$ is a subspace of $\real^p$, then 
 
 $$
 \begin{aligned}
 \dim W + \dim W^\perp = p.
 \end{aligned}
 $$

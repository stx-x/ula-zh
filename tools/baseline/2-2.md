# 2.2 Matrix multiplication and linear combinations

The previous section introduced vectors and linear combinations and demonstrated how they provide a way to think about linear systems geometrically. In particular, we saw that the vector $\bvec$ is a linear combination of the vectors $\vvec_1,\vvec_2,\ldots,\vvec_n$ precisely when the linear system corresponding to the augmented matrix 

$$
\left[\begin{array}{rrrr|r}
\vvec_1 & \vvec_2 & \cdots & \vvec_n & \bvec
\end{array}\right]
$$

 is consistent.

Our goal in this section is to introduce matrix multiplication, another algebraic operation that deepens the connection between linear systems and linear combinations.

We first thought of a matrix as a rectangular array of numbers. If we say that the *shape* of a matrix is $m\times
n$, we mean that it has $m$ rows and $n$ columns. For instance, the shape of the matrix below is $3\times4$: 

$$
\left[
\begin{array}{rrrr}
0 & 4 & -3 & 1 \\
3 & -1 & 2 & 0 \\
2 & 0 & -1 & 1 \\
\end{array}
\right]
$$

.

We may also think of the columns of a matrix as a set of vectors. For instance, the matrix above may be represented as 

$$
\left[
\begin{array}{rrrr}
\vvec_1 & \vvec_2 & \vvec_3 & \vvec_4
\end{array}
\right]
$$

 where 

$$
\vvec_1=\left[\begin{array}{r}0\\3\\2\\ \end{array}\right],
\vvec_2=\left[\begin{array}{r}4\\-1\\0\\ \end{array}\right],
\vvec_3=\left[\begin{array}{r}-3\\2\\-1\\ \end{array}\right],
\vvec_4=\left[\begin{array}{r}1\\0\\1\\ \end{array}\right]
$$

. In this way, we see that the $3\times 4$ matrix is equivalent to an ordered set of 4 vectors in $\real^3$.

This means that we may define scalar multiplication and matrix addition operations using the corresponding column-wise vector operations. For instance, 

$$
\begin{aligned}
c\left[\begin{array}{rrrr}
\vvec_1 & \vvec_2 & \cdots & \vvec_n
\end{array}
\right]
{}={} &
\left[\begin{array}{rrrr}
c\vvec_1 & c\vvec_2 & \cdots & c\vvec_n
\end{array}
\right] \\
\left[\begin{array}{rrrr}
\vvec_1 & \vvec_2 & \cdots & \vvec_n
\end{array}
\right]
{}+{} &
\left[\begin{array}{rrrr}
\wvec_1 & \wvec_2 & \cdots & \wvec_n
\end{array}
\right]
\\
{}={} &
\left[\begin{array}{rrrr}
\vvec_1+\wvec_1 & \vvec_2+\wvec_2 & \cdots &
\vvec_n+\wvec_n
\end{array}
\right]. \\
\end{aligned}
$$

As this preview activity shows, the operations of scalar multiplication and addition of matrices are natural extensions of their vector counterparts. Some care, however, is required when adding matrices. Since we need the same number of vectors to add and since those vectors must be of the same dimension, two matrices must have the same shape if we wish to form their sum.

A more important operation will be matrix multiplication as it allows us to compactly express linear systems. We now introduce the product of a matrix and a vector with an example.

<a id="2-2-example-1" class="ula-anchor"></a>

:::::: example 例 2.2.1 · Matrix-vector multiplication

Suppose we have the matrix $A$ and vector $\xvec$: 

$$
A = \left[\begin{array}{rr}
-2 & 3 \\
0 & 2 \\
3 & 1 \\
\end{array}\right],~~~
\xvec = \left[\begin{array}{r}
2 \\ 3 \\ \end{array}\right]
$$

. Their product will be defined to be the linear combination of the columns of $A$ using the components of $\xvec$ as weights. This means that 

$$
\begin{aligned}
A\xvec =
\left[\begin{array}{rr}
-2 & 3 \\
0 & 2 \\
3 & 1 \\
\end{array}\right]
\left[\begin{array}{r} 2 \\ 3 \\ \end{array}\right]
{}={} &
2 \left[\begin{array}{r} -2 \\ 0 \\ 3 \\ \end{array}\right] +
3 \left[\begin{array}{r} 3 \\ 2 \\ 1 \\ \end{array}\right]
\\ \\
{}={} &
\left[\begin{array}{r} -4 \\ 0 \\ 6 \\ \end{array}\right] +
\left[\begin{array}{r} 9 \\ 6 \\ 3 \\ \end{array}\right] \\ \\
{}={} &
\left[\begin{array}{r} 5 \\ 6 \\ 9 \\ \end{array}\right]. \\
\end{aligned}
$$

Because $A$ has two columns, we need two weights to form a linear combination of those columns, which means that $\xvec$ must have two components. In other words, the number of columns of $A$ must equal the dimension of the vector $\xvec$.

Similarly, the columns of $A$ are 3-dimensional so any linear combination of them is 3-dimensional as well. Therefore, $A\xvec$ will be 3-dimensional.

We then see that if $A$ is a $3\times2$ matrix, $\xvec$ must be a 2-dimensional vector and $A\xvec$ will be 3-dimensional.

::::::

More generally, we have the following definition.

<a id="2-2-definition-1" class="ula-anchor"></a>

:::::: definition 定义 2.2.1 · Matrix-vector multiplication

The product of a matrix $A$ by a vector $\xvec$ will be the linear combination of the columns of $A$ using the components of $\xvec$ as weights. More specifically, if 

$$
A=\left[\begin{array}{rrrr}
\vvec_1 & \vvec_2 & \ldots & \vvec_n
\end{array}\right],~~~
\xvec = \left[\begin{array}{r}
c_1 \\ c_2 \\ \vdots \\ c_n \end{array}\right],
$$

 then 

$$
\begin{aligned}
A\xvec = c_1\vvec_1 + c_2\vvec_2 + \ldots + c_n\vvec_n
\end{aligned}
$$

.

If $A$ is an $m\times n$ matrix, then $\xvec$ must be an $n$-dimensional vector, and the product $A\xvec$ will be an $m$-dimensional vector.

Multiplication of a matrix $A$ and a vector is defined as a linear combination of the columns of $A$. However, there is a shortcut for computing such a product. Let's look at our previous example and focus on the first row of the product. 

$$
\left[\begin{array}{rr}
-2 & 3 \\
0 & 2 \\
3 & 1 \\
\end{array}\right]
\left[\begin{array}{r} 2 \\ 3 \\ \end{array}\right]
=
2 \left[\begin{array}{r} -2 \\ * \\ * \\ \end{array}\right] +
3 \left[\begin{array}{r} 3 \\ * \\ * \\ \end{array}\right]
=
\left[\begin{array}{c} 2(-2)+3(3) \\ * \\ * \\ \end{array}\right]
=
\left[\begin{array}{r} 5 \\ * \\ * \\ \end{array}\right]
$$

.

To find the first component of the product, we consider the first row of the matrix. We then multiply the first entry in that row by the first component of the vector, the second entry by the second component of the vector, and so on, and add the results. In this way, we see that the third component of the product would be obtained from the third row of the matrix by computing $2(3) + 3(1) = 9$.

This activity demonstrates several general properties satisfied by matrix multiplication that we record here.

<a id="prop-matrix-mult-prop" class="ula-anchor"></a>

:::::: proposition 命题 2.2.1 · Linearity of matrix multiplication

If $A$ is a matrix, $\vvec$ and $\wvec$ vectors of the appropriate dimensions, and $c$ a scalar, then 

- $A\zerovec = \zerovec$.
- $A(c\vvec) = cA\vvec$.
- $A(\vvec+\wvec) = A\vvec + A\wvec$.

::::::

So far, we have begun with a matrix $A$ and a vector $\xvec$ and formed their product $A\xvec = \bvec$. We would now like to turn this around: beginning with a matrix $A$ and a vector $\bvec$, we will ask if we can find a vector $\xvec$ such that $A\xvec = \bvec$. This will naturally lead back to linear systems.

To see the connection between the matrix equation $A\xvec =
\bvec$ and linear systems, let's write the matrix $A$ in terms of its columns $\vvec_i$ and $\xvec$ in terms of its components. 

$$
A = \left[
\begin{array}{rrrr}
\vvec_1 & \vvec_2 & \ldots \vvec_n
\end{array}
\right],
\xvec = \left[
\begin{array}{c}
c_1 \\ c_2 \\ \vdots \\ c_n \\
\end{array}
\right]
$$

.

We know that the matrix product $A\xvec$ forms a linear combination of the columns of $A$. Therefore, the equation $A\xvec = \bvec$ is merely a compact way of writing the equation for the weights $c_i$: 

$$
\begin{aligned}
c_1\vvec_1 + c_2\vvec_2 + \ldots + c_n\vvec_n = \bvec
\end{aligned}
$$

. We have seen this equation before: Remember that [命题 2.1.1](/chapter-2/2-1#prop-system-comb) says that the solutions of this equation are the same as the solutions to the linear system whose augmented matrix is 

$$
\left[\begin{array}{rrrr|r}
\vvec_1 & \vvec_2 & \ldots & \vvec_n & \bvec
\end{array}\right]
$$

.

This gives us three different ways of looking at the same solution space.

<a id="prop-matrix-eq-solution" class="ula-anchor"></a>

:::::: proposition 命题 2.2.2

If 

$$
A=\left[\begin{array}{rrrr}
\vvec_1&\vvec_2&\ldots\vvec_n
\end{array}\right]
$$

 and 

$$
\xvec=\left[
\begin{array}{c}
x_1 \\ x_2 \\ \vdots \\ x_n \\
\end{array}\right]
$$

, then the following statements are equivalent. 

- The vector $\xvec$ satisfies the equation $A\xvec = \bvec$.
- The vector $\bvec$ is a linear combination of the columns of $A$ with weights $x_j$: 
 
 $$
 \begin{aligned}
 x_1\vvec_1 + x_2\vvec_2 + \ldots + x_n\vvec_n = \bvec
 \end{aligned}
 $$
 
 .
- The components of $\xvec$ form a solution to the linear system corresponding to the augmented matrix 
 
 $$
 \left[\begin{array}{rrrr|r}
 \vvec_1 & \vvec_2 & \cdots & \vvec_n & \bvec
 \end{array}\right]\text{.}
 $$

::::::

When the matrix 

$$
A = \left[\begin{array}{rrrr}
\vvec_1&\vvec_2&\cdots&\vvec_n\end{array}\right]
$$

, we will frequently write 

$$
\left[\begin{array}{rrrr|r}
\vvec_1&\vvec_2&\cdots&\vvec_n&\bvec\end{array}\right]
= \left[ \begin{array}{r|r} A & \bvec \end{array}\right]
$$

 and say that the matrix $A$ is augmented by the vector $\bvec$.

The equation $A\xvec = \bvec$ gives a notationally compact way to write a linear system. Moreover, this notation will allow us to focus on important features of the system that determine its solution space.

<a id="2-2-example-2" class="ula-anchor"></a>

:::::: example 例 2.2.2

We will describe the solution space of the equation 

$$
\left[\begin{array}{rrr}
2 & 0 & 2 \\
4 & -1 & 6 \\
1 & 3 & -5 \\
\end{array}\right]
\xvec
=
\left[\begin{array}{r}
0 \\ -5 \\ 15
\end{array}\right].
$$

By [命题 2.2.2](/chapter-2/2-2#prop-matrix-eq-solution), this equation may be equivalently expressed as 

$$
x_1\left[\begin{array}{r}2\\4\\1\end{array}\right] +
x_2\left[\begin{array}{r}0\\-1\\3\end{array}\right]+
x_3\left[\begin{array}{r}2\\6\\-5\end{array}\right]=
\left[\begin{array}{r}0\\-5\\15\end{array}\right]
$$

, which is the linear system corresponding to the augmented matrix 

$$
\left[\begin{array}{rrr|r}
2 & 0 & 2 & 0 \\
4 & -1 & 6 & -5 \\
1 & 3 & -5 & 15 \\
\end{array} \right]
$$

. The reduced row echelon form of the augmented matrix is 

$$
\left[\begin{array}{rrr|r}
2 & 0 & 2 & 0 \\
4 & -1 & 6 & -5 \\
1 & 3 & -5 & 15 \\
\end{array} \right]
\sim
\left[\begin{array}{rrr|r}
1 & 0 & 1 & 0 \\
0 & 1 & -2 & 5 \\
0 & 0 & 0 & 0 \\
\end{array} \right],
$$

 which corresponds to the linear system 

$$
\begin{alignedat}{4}
x_1 & & & {}+{} & x_3 & {}={} & 0 \\
& & x_2 & {}-{} & 2x_3 & {}={} & 5. \\
\end{alignedat}
$$

 The variable $x_3$ is free so we may write the solution space parametrically as 

$$
\begin{aligned}
x_1 & {}={} -x_3 \\
x_2 & {}={} 5+2x_3. \\
\end{aligned}
$$

Since we originally asked to describe the solutions to the equation $A\xvec = \bvec$, we will express the solution in terms of the vector $\xvec$: 

$$
\xvec
=\left[
\begin{array}{r}
x_1 \\ x_2 \\ x_3
\end{array}
\right]
=
\left[
\begin{array}{r}
-x_3 \\ 5 + 2x_3 \\ x_3
\end{array}
\right]
=\left[\begin{array}{r}0\\5\\0\end{array}\right]
+x_3\left[\begin{array}{r}-1\\2\\1\end{array}\right]
$$

 As before, we call this a parametric description of the solution space.

This shows that the solutions $\xvec$ may be written in the form $\vvec + x_3\wvec$, for appropriate vectors $\vvec$ and $\wvec$. Geometrically, the solution space is a line in $\real^3$ through $\vvec$ moving parallel to $\wvec$.

In this section, we have developed some algebraic operations on matrices with the aim of simplifying our description of linear systems. We now introduce a final operation, the product of two matrices, that will become important when we study linear transformations in 第 2.5 节.

<a id="2-2-definition-2" class="ula-anchor"></a>

:::::: definition 定义 2.2.2 · Matrix-matrix multiplication

Given matrices $A$ and $B$, we form their product $AB$ by first writing $B$ in terms of its columns 

$$
B = \left[\begin{array}{rrrr}
\vvec_1 & \vvec_2 & \cdots & \vvec_p
\end{array}\right]
$$

 and then defining 

$$
AB = \left[\begin{array}{rrrr}
A\vvec_1 & A\vvec_2 & \cdots & A\vvec_p
\end{array}\right].
$$

::::::

<a id="2-2-example-3" class="ula-anchor"></a>

:::::: example 例 2.2.3

Given the matrices 

$$
A = \left[\begin{array}{rr}
4 & 2 \\
0 & 1 \\
-3 & 4 \\
2 & 0 \\
\end{array}\right],~~~
B = \left[\begin{array}{rrr}
-2 & 3 & 0 \\
1 & 2 & -2 \\
\end{array}\right]
$$

, we have 

$$
AB = \left[\begin{array}{rrr}
A \twovec{-2}{1} &
A \twovec{3}{2} &
A \twovec{0}{-2}
\end{array}\right]
= \left[\begin{array}{rrr}
-6 & 16 & -4 \\
1 & 2 & -2 \\
10 & -1 & -8 \\
-4 & 6 & 0
\end{array}\right]
$$

.

:::::: observation 观察 2.2.1

It is important to note that we can only multiply matrices if the shapes of the matrices are compatible. More specifically, when constructing the product $AB$, the matrix $A$ multiplies the columns of $B$. Therefore, the number of columns of $A$ must equal the number of rows of $B$. When this condition is met, the number of rows of $AB$ is the number of rows of $A$, and the number of columns of $AB$ is the number of columns of $B$.

This activity demonstrated some general properties about products of matrices, which mirror some properties about operations with real numbers.

:::::: note Properties of Matrix-matrix Multiplication

If $A$, $B$, and $C$ are matrices such that the following operations are defined, it follows that 

::: ula-dl
**Associativity:** — $A(BC) = (AB)C$.

**Distributivity:** — $A(B+C) = AB+AC$.

$(A+B)C = AC+BC$.
:::

::::::

At the same time, there are a few properties that hold for real numbers that do not hold for matrices.

:::::: note Caution

The following properties hold for real numbers but not for matrices. 

::: ula-dl
**Commutativity:** — It is *not* generally true that $AB = BA$.

**Cancellation:** — It is *not* generally true that $AB = AC$ implies that $B = C$.

**Zero divisors:** — It is *not* generally true that $AB = 0$ implies that either $A=0$ or $B=0$.
:::

In this section, we have found an especially simple way to express linear systems using matrix multiplication. 

- If $A$ is an $m\times n$ matrix and $\xvec$ an $n$-dimensional vector, then $A\xvec$ is the linear combination of the columns of $A$ using the components of $\xvec$ as weights. The vector $A\xvec$ is $m$-dimensional.
- The solution space to the equation $A\xvec =
 \bvec$ is the same as the solution space to the linear system corresponding to the augmented matrix 
 
 $$
 \left[ \begin{array}{r|r} A & \bvec \end{array}\right]
 $$
 
 .
- If $A$ is an $m\times n$ matrix and $B$ is an $n\times p$ matrix, we can form the product $AB$, which is an $m\times p$ matrix whose columns are the products of $A$ and the columns of $B$.

# 4.4 Dynamical systems

The last section demonstrated ways in which we may relate a matrix, and the effect that multiplication has on vectors, to a simpler form. For instance, if there is a basis of $\real^n$ consisting of eigenvectors of $A$, we saw that $A$ is similar to a diagonal matrix $D$. As a result, the effect of multiplying vectors by $A$, when expressed using the basis of eigenvectors, is the same as multiplying by $D$.

In this section, we will put these ideas to use as we explore discrete dynamical systems, first encountered in [Discrete Dynamical Systems](/chapter-2/2-5#subsec-dynamical-systems). Recall that we used a state vector $\xvec$ to characterize the state of some system at a particular time, such as the distribution of delivery trucks between two locations. A matrix $A$ described the transition of the state vector with $A\xvec$ characterizing the state of the system at a later time. Since we would like to understand how the state vector evolves over time, we are interested in studying the sequence of vectors $A^k\xvec$.

Our goal in this section is to describe the types of behaviors that dynamical systems exhibit and to develop a means of detecting these behaviors.

We will begin with a dynamical system that illustrates how the ideas we've been developing can help us understand the populations of two interacting species. There are several possible ways in which two species may interact. For example, wolves on Isle Royale in northern Michigan prey on moose so this interaction is often called a predator-prey relationship. Other interactions between species, such as bees and flowering plants, are mutually beneficial for both species.

Suppose we have two species $R$ and $S$ that interact with each another and that we record the change in their populations from year to year. When we begin our study, the populations, measured in thousands, are $R_0$ and $S_0$; after $k$ years, the populations are $R_k$ and $S_k$.

If we know the populations in one year, suppose that the populations in the following year are determined by the expressions 

$$
\begin{aligned}
R_{k+1} & {}={} 0.9 R_k + 0.8 S_k \\
S_{k+1} & {}={} 0.2 R_k + 0.9 S_k\text{.} \\
\end{aligned}
$$

 This is an example of a mutually beneficial relationship between two species. If species $S$ is not present, then $R_{k+1} = 0.9R_k$, which means that the population of species $R$ decreases every year. However, species $R$ benefits from the presence of species $S$, which helps $R$ to grow by 80% of the population of species $S$. In the same way, $S$ benefits from the presence of $R$.

We will record the populations in a vector $\xvec_k = \twovec{R_k}{S_k}$ and note that $\xvec_{k+1} = A\xvec_k$ where 

$$
A = \left[\begin{array}{rr}
0.9 & 0.8 \\
0.2 & 0.9 \\
\end{array}\right]
$$

.

This activity demonstrates the type of systems we will be considering. In particular, we will have vectors $\xvec_k$ that describe the state of the system at time $k$ and a matrix $A$ that describes how the state evolves from one time to the next: $\xvec_{k+1} = A\xvec_k$. The eigenvalues and eigenvectors of $A$ provide the key that helps us understand how the vectors $\xvec_k$ evolve and that enables us to make long-range predictions.

Let's look at the specific example in the previous activity more carefully. We see that 

$$
\xvec_{k+1} = A\xvec_k =
\left[\begin{array}{rr}
0.9 & 0.8 \\
0.2 & 0.9 \\
\end{array}\right]
\xvec_k
$$

 and that the matrix $A$ has eigenvectors $\vvec_1 =
\twovec{2}{1}$ and $\vvec_2=\twovec{-2}{1}$ with associated eigenvalues $\lambda_1=1.3$ and $\lambda_2=0.5$.

With initial populations $\xvec_0=\twovec{2}{3}$, we have 

$$
\begin{aligned}
\xvec_0 & {}={} 2\vvec_1 + 1\vvec_2 \\
\xvec_1 & {}={} 1.3\cdot2\vvec_1 + 0.5\cdot1\vvec_2 \\
\xvec_2 & {}={} 1.3^2\cdot2\vvec_1 + 0.5^2\cdot1\vvec_2 \\
\xvec_k & {}={} 1.3^k\cdot2\vvec_1 + 0.5^k\cdot1\vvec_2. \\
\end{aligned}
$$

Let's shift our perspective slightly. The eigenvectors $\vvec_1$ and $\vvec_2$ form a basis $\bcal$ of $\real^2$, which says that $A$ is diagonalizable; that is, $A = PDP^{-1}$ where 

$$
P = \left[\begin{array}{rr} \vvec_1 & \vvec_2
\end{array}\right] =
\left[\begin{array}{rr}
2 & -2 \\
1 & 1 \\
\end{array}\right], \qquad
D = \left[\begin{array}{rr}
1.3 & 0 \\
0 & 0.5 \\
\end{array}\right].
$$

The coordinate system defined by the basis $\bcal$ can be used to express the state vectors. For instance, we can write the initial state vector $\xvec_0=\twovec{2}{3}= 2\vvec_1+\vvec_2$, which means that $\coords{\xvec_0}{\bcal} = \twovec{2}{1}$. Moreover, $\xvec_1=A\xvec_0 = (1.3)\cdot2\vvec_1 + (0.5)\cdot1\vvec_2$ so that 

$$
\begin{aligned}
\coords{\xvec_1}{\bcal} = \twovec{1.3\cdot 2}{0.5\cdot 1} =
D\twovec21 = D\coords{\xvec_0}{\bcal}.
\end{aligned}
$$

 In the same way, 

$$
\begin{aligned}
\coords{\xvec_1}{\bcal} & {}={} D\coords{\xvec_0}{\bcal} =
\ctwovec{1.3\cdot 2}{0.5\cdot 1} \\
\coords{\xvec_2}{\bcal} & {}={} D\coords{\xvec_1}{\bcal} =
\ctwovec{1.3^2\cdot 2}{0.5^2\cdot1} \\
\coords{\xvec_3}{\bcal} & {}={} D\coords{\xvec_2}{\bcal} =
\ctwovec{1.3^3\cdot2}{0.5^3\cdot1} \\
\coords{\xvec_k}{\bcal} & {}={}
\ctwovec{1.3^k\cdot2}{0.5^k\cdot1}\text{.} \\
\end{aligned}
$$

More generally, we have 

$$
\begin{aligned}
\coords{A\xvec}{\bcal} = D\coords{\xvec}{\bcal},
\end{aligned}
$$

 which is a restatement of the fact that $A$ is similar to $D$.

::: ula-sbs
Thinking about this geometrically, we begin with the vector $\coords{\xvec_0}{\bcal}=\ctwovec{2}{1}$. Subsequent vectors $\coords{\xvec_k}{\bcal}$ are obtained by scaling horizontally by a factor of $1.3$ and scaling vertically by a factor $0.5$. Notice how the points move along a curve away from the origin becoming ever closer to the horizontal axis. After a very long time, $\coords{\xvec_k}{\bcal} \approx
\ctwovec{1.3^k\cdot2}{0}$.

![](/figures/ch4/pf-eigen-dynamical-intro.svg)
:::

::: ula-sbs
To recover the behavior of the sequence $\xvec_0, \xvec_1,
\xvec_2, \ldots$, we change coordinate systems using the basis defined by $\vvec_1$ and $\vvec_2$. Here, the points move along a curve away from the origin becoming ever closer to the line defined by $\vvec_1$.

![](/figures/ch4/pf-eigen-dynamical-intro-1.svg)
:::

Eventually, the vectors become practically indistinguishable from a scalar multiple of $\vvec_1 = \twovec{2}{1}$ since $\xvec_k\approx 1.3^k\cdot2\vvec_1$. This means that 

$$
\begin{aligned}
\xvec_k = \twovec{R_k}{S_k} \approx 1.3^k\cdot 2\vvec_1 =
\twovec{1.3^k\cdot4}{1.3^k\cdot2}.
\end{aligned}
$$

 This shows that 

$$
\begin{aligned}
R_k/S_k \approx (1.3^k\cdot 4) / (1.3^k\cdot 2) = 2
\end{aligned}
$$

 so that we expect the population of species $R$ to eventually be about twice that of species $S$.

In addition, $\xvec_{k+1}
\approx 1.3\xvec_k$ so that $R_{k+1}\approx 1.3 R_k$ and $S_{k+1}\approx 1.3 S_k$, which tells us that both populations are multiplied by 1.3 every year meaning the annual growth rate for both populations is about 30%.

In the same way, we can consider other possible initial populations $\xvec_0$ as shown in [图 4.4.1](/chapter-4/4-4#fig-eigen-dynam-saddle). Regardless of $\xvec_0$, the population vectors, in the coordinates defined by $\bcal$, are scaled horizontally by a factor of $1.3$ and vertically by a factor of $0.5$. The sequence of points $\coords{\xvec_k}{\bcal}$, called *trajectories*, move along the curves, as shown on the left. In the standard coordinate system, we see that the trajectories converge to the eigenspace $E_{1.3}$.

We conclude that, regardless of the initial populations, the ratio of the populations $R_k/S_k$ will approach 2 to 1 and that the growth rate for both populations approaches 30%. This example demonstrates the power of using eigenvalues and eigenvectors to rewrite the problem in terms of a new coordinate system. By doing so, we are able to predict the long-term behavior of the populations independently of the initial populations.

Diagrams like those shown in [图 4.4.1](/chapter-4/4-4#fig-eigen-dynam-saddle) are called *phase portraits.* On the left of [图 4.4.1](/chapter-4/4-4#fig-eigen-dynam-saddle) is the phase portrait of the diagonal matrix $D=\mattwo{1.3}00{0.5}$ while the right of that figure shows the phase portrait of $A =
\mattwo{0.9}{0.8}{0.2}{0.9}$. The phase portrait of $D$ is relatively easy to understand because it is determined only by the two eigenvalues. Once we have the phase portrait of $D$, however, the phase portrait of $A$ has a similar appearance with the eigenvectors $\vvec_j$ replacing the standard basis vectors $\evec_j$.

In the previous example, we were able to make predictions about the behavior of trajectories $\xvec_k=A^k\xvec_0$ by considering the eigenvalues and eigenvectors of the matrix $A$. The next activity looks at a collection of matrices that demonstrate the types of behavior a $2\times2$ dynamical system can exhibit.

We have been exploring discrete dynamical systems in which an initial state vector $\xvec_0$ evolves over time according to the rule $\xvec_{k+1}=A\xvec_k$. The eigenvalues and eigenvectors of $A$ help us understand the behavior of the state vectors. In the $2\times2$ case, we saw that 

- $|\lambda_1|, |\lambda_2| \lt 1$ produces an attractor so that trajectories are pulled in toward the origin.
- $|\lambda_1| \gt 1$ and $|\lambda_2| \lt 1$ produces a saddle in which most trajectories are pushed away from the origin and in the direction of $E_{\lambda_1}$.
- $|\lambda_1|, |\lambda_2| \gt 1$ produces a repellor in which trajectories are pushed away from the origin.

 The same kind of reasoning allows us to analyze $n\times n$ systems as well.

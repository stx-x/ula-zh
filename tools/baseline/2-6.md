# 2.6 The geometry of matrix transformations

Matrix transformations, which we explored in the last section, allow us to describe certain functions $T:\real^n\to\real^m$. In this section, we will demonstrate how matrix transformations provide a convenient way to describe geometric operations, such as rotations, reflections, and scalings. We will then explore how matrix transformations are used in computer animation.

The previous activity presented some examples showing that matrix transformations can perform interesting geometric operations, such as rotations, scalings, and reflections. Before we go any further, we should explain why it is possible to represent these operations by matrix transformations. In fact, we ask more generally: what types of functions $T:\real^n\to\real^m$ are represented as matrix transformations?

The [linearity of matrix-vector multiplication](/chapter-2/2-2#prop-matrix-mult-prop) provides the key to answering this question. Remember that if $A$ is a matrix, $\vvec$ and $\wvec$ vectors, and $c$ a scalar, then 

$$
\begin{aligned}
A(c\vvec) & {}={} cA\vvec \\
A(\vvec + \wvec) & {}={} A\vvec + A\wvec\text{.}
\end{aligned}
$$

. This means that a matrix transformation $T(\xvec) = A\xvec$ satisfies the corresponding linearity property:

<a id="linearity-of-linear-transformations" class="ula-anchor"></a>

:::::: note Linearity of Matrix Transformations

$$
\begin{aligned}
T(c\vvec) & {}={} cT(\vvec) \\
T(\vvec + \wvec) & {}={} T(\vvec) + T(\wvec)\text{.}
\end{aligned}
$$

::::::

It turns out that, if $T:\real^n\to\real^m$ satisfies these two linearity properties, then we can find a matrix $A$ such that $T(\xvec) = A\xvec$. In fact, [命题 2.5.1](/chapter-2/2-5#prop-linear-trans-columns) tells us how to form $A$; we simply write 

$$
A = \left[\begin{array}{rrrr}
T(\evec_1) & T(\evec_2) & \ldots & T(\evec_n)
\end{array}\right]
$$

. We will now check that $T(\xvec) = A\xvec$ using the linearity of $T$: 

$$
\begin{aligned}
T(\xvec) = T\left(\fourvec{x_1}{x_2}{\vdots}{x_n}\right)
& {}={}
T(x_1\evec_1 + x_2\evec_2 + \ldots + x_n\evec_n) \\ \\
& {}={}
x_1T(\evec_1) + x_2T(\evec_2) + \ldots + x_nT(\evec_n) \\ \\
& {}={}
x_1A\evec_1 + x_2A\evec_2 + \ldots + x_nA\evec_n \\ \\
& {}={}
A(x_1\evec_1 + x_2\evec_2 + \ldots + x_n\evec_n) \\ \\
& {}={}
A\fourvec{x_1}{x_2}{\vdots}{x_n} \\ \\
& {}={}
A\xvec
\end{aligned}
$$

.

The result is the following proposition.

<a id="prop-linear-trans-characterization" class="ula-anchor"></a>

:::::: proposition 命题 2.6.1

The function $T:\real^n\to\real^m$ is a matrix transformation where $T(\xvec) = A\xvec$ for some $m\times n$ matrix $A$ if and only if 

$$
\begin{aligned}
T(c\vvec) & {}={} cT(\vvec) \\
T(\vvec + \wvec) & {}={} T(\vvec) + T(\wvec)\text{.}
\end{aligned}
$$

. In this case, $A$ is the matrix whose columns are $T(\evec_j)$; that is, 

$$
A=\left[\begin{array}{rrrr}
T(\evec_1) & T(\evec_2) & \ldots & T(\evec_n)
\end{array}\right]
$$

.

::::::

Said simply, this proposition means that if we have a function $T:\real^n\to\real^m$ and can verify the two linearity properties stated in the proposition, then we know that $T$ is a matrix transformation. Let's see how this works in practice.

:::::: example 例 2.6.1

We will consider the function $T:\real^2\to\real^2$ that rotates a vector $\xvec$ by $45^\circ$ in the counterclockwise direction to obtain $T(\xvec)$ as seen in fig-45-rotation.

We first need to know that $T$ can be represented by a matrix transformation, which means, by [命题 2.6.1](/chapter-2/2-6#prop-linear-trans-characterization), that we need to verify the linearity properties: 

$$
\begin{aligned}
T(c\vvec) & {}={} cT(\vvec) \\
T(\vvec + \wvec) & {}={} T(\vvec) + T(\wvec)\text{.}
\end{aligned}
$$

The next two figures illustrate why these properties hold. For instance, fig-45-rotation-linear-mult shows the relationship between $T(\vvec)$ and $T(c\vvec)$ when $c$ is a scalar. In particular, scaling a vector and then rotating it is the same as rotating and then scaling it, which means that $T(c\vvec)

We see that the vector $T(c\vvec)$ is a scalar multiple to $T(\vvec)$ so that $T(c\vvec) =
cT(\vvec)$.

::::

Similarly, fig-45-rotation-linear-add shows the relationship between $T(\vvec+\wvec)$, $T(\vvec)$, and $T(\wvec)$. Remember that the sum of two vectors is represented by the diagonal of the parallelogram defined by the two vectors. The rotation $T$ has the effect of rotating the parallelogram defined by $\vvec$ and $\wvec$ into the parallelogram defined by $T(\vvec)$ and $T(\wvec)$, explaining why $T(\vvec+\wvec) = T(\vvec) + T(\wvec)$.

<a id="fig-45-rotation-linear-add" class="ula-anchor"></a>

We see that the vector $T(\vvec+\wvec)$ is the sum of $T(\vvec)$ and $T(\wvec)$ so that $T(\vvec + \wvec) = T(\vvec) + T(\wvec)$.

::::

Having verified these two properties, we now know that the function $T$ that rotates vectors by $45^\circ$ is a matrix transformation. We may therefore write it as $T(\xvec) = A\xvec$ where $A$ is the $2\times2$ matrix 

$$
A=\left[\begin{array}{rr} T(\evec_1) & T(\evec_2)
\end{array}\right]
$$

. The columns of this matrix, $T(\evec_1)$ and $T(\evec_2)$, are shown on the right of fig-45-rotation-basis.

<a id="fig-45-rotation-basis" class="ula-anchor"></a>

:::: ula-figure

::: ula-sbs
![Two standard vectors e one and e two](/figures/ch2/pf-45-rotation-basis-a.svg)

![The result of applying the matrix transformation T to e one and e two](/figures/ch2/pf-45-rotation-basis-b.svg)
:::

The matrix transformation $T$ rotates $\evec_1$ and $\evec_2$ by $45^\circ$.

::::

Notice that $T(\evec_1)$ forms an isosceles right triangle, as shown in fig-45-rotation-e1. Since the length of $\evec_1$ is 1, the length of $T(\evec_1)$, the hypotenuse of the triangle, is also 1, and by Pythagoras' theorem, the lengths of its legs are $1/\sqrt{2}$.

<a id="fig-45-rotation-e1" class="ula-anchor"></a>

:::: ula-figure

![The vector obtained by rotating e 1 counterclockwise by 45 degrees.](/figures/ch2/pf-45-rotation-e1.svg)

The vector $T(\evec_1)$ has length 1 and is the hypotenuse of a right isosceles triangle.

::::

This leads to $T(\evec_1) = \twovec{\frac1{\sqrt{2}}}
{\frac1{\sqrt{2}}}$. In the same way, we find that $T(\evec_2) = \twovec{-\frac1{\sqrt{2}}}
{\frac1{\sqrt{2}}}$ so that the matrix $A$ is 

$$
A = \left[\begin{array}{rr}
\frac{1}{\sqrt{2}} &
-\frac{1}{\sqrt{2}} \\
\frac{1}{\sqrt{2}} &
\frac{1}{\sqrt{2}} \\
\end{array}\right]
$$

. You may wish to check this using the interactive diagram in the previous activity using the approximation $1/\sqrt{2} \approx 0.7$.

::::::

In this example, we found that $T$, a function describing a rotation in the plane, was in fact a matrix transformation by checking that 

$$
\begin{aligned}
T(c\vvec) & {}={} cT(\vvec) \\
T(\vvec + \wvec) & {}={} T(\vvec) + T(\wvec)\text{.}
\end{aligned}.
$$

 The same kind of thinking applies more generally to show that rotations, reflections, and scalings are matrix transformations. Similarly, we could revisit the functions in [活动 2.5.3](/chapter-2/2-5#activity-mt-intro) and verify that they are matrix transformations.

This section explored how geometric operations are performed by matrix transformations. 

- A function $T:\real^n\to\real^m$ is a matrix transformation if and only if these properties are satisfied: 
 
 $$
 \begin{aligned}
 T(c\vvec) & = cT(\vvec) \\
 T(\vvec+\wvec) & = T(\vvec)+T(\wvec). \\
 \end{aligned}
 $$
- Geometric operations, such as rotations, reflections, and scalings, can be represented as matrix transformations.
- Composing geometric operations corresponds to matrix multiplication.
- Computer animators use homogeneous coordinates and matrix transformations to create the illusion of motion.

# 3.2 Bases and coordinate systems

In this section, we will develop the concept of a *basis* through which we will create new coordinate systems in $\real^m$. We will see that the right choice of a coordinate system provides a more natural way to approach some problems.

Standard Cartesian coordinates are commonly used to describe points in the plane. If we mention the point $(4,3)$, we know that we arrive at this point from the origin by moving four units to the right and three units up.

Sometimes, however, it is more natural to work in a different coordinate system. Suppose that you live in the city whose map is shown in [图 3.2.1](/chapter-3/3-2#fig-city-map) and that you would like to give a guest directions for getting from your house to the store. You would probably say something like, "Go four blocks up Maple. Then turn left on Main for three blocks." The grid of streets in the city gives a more natural coordinate system than standard north-south, east-west coordinates. We will see how a basis provides the right framework for this type of thinking.

We can develop a test to determine if a set of vectors $\vvec_1,\vvec_2,\ldots,\vvec_n$ forms a basis for $\real^m$ by considering the matrix 

$$
A = \left[\begin{array}{rrrr}
\vvec_1 & \vvec_2 & \ldots & \vvec_n
\end{array}\right]
$$

. To be a basis, this set of vectors must span $\real^m$ and be linearly independent.

We know that the span of the set of vectors is $\real^m$ if and only if $A$ has a pivot position in every row. We also know that the set of vectors is linearly independent if and only if $A$ has a pivot position in every column. This means that a set of vectors forms a basis if and only if $A$ has a pivot in every row and every column. Therefore, $A$ must be row equivalent to the identity matrix $I$: 

$$
A \sim \left[\begin{array}{cccc}
1 & 0 & \cdots & 0 \\
0 & 1 & \cdots & 0 \\
\vdots & \vdots & \ddots & \vdots \\
0 & 0 & \cdots & 1
\end{array}\right] = I
$$

.

In addition to helping identify bases, this fact tells us something important about the number of vectors in a basis. Since the matrix $A$ has a pivot position in every row and every column, it must have the same number of rows as columns. Therefore, the number of vectors in a basis for $\real^m$ must be $m$. For example, a basis for $\real^{23}$ must have exactly 23 vectors.

<a id="3-2-proposition-1" class="ula-anchor"></a>

:::::: proposition 命题 3.2.1

A set of vectors forms a basis for $\real^m$ if and only if the matrix 

$$
A = \left[\begin{array}{rrrr}
\vvec_1 & \vvec_2 & \cdots & \vvec_n
\end{array}\right]
\sim I.
$$

 This means there must be $m$ vectors in a basis for $\real^m$.

::::::

:::::: example 例 3.2.1

Notice that the vectors 

$$
\begin{aligned}
\evec_1 = \threevec{1}{0}{0},

\evec_3 = \threevec{0}{0}{1}

 form the columns of the $3\times3$ identity matrix, which implies that this set forms a basis for $\real^3$. More generally, the set of vectors $\evec_1,\evec_2,\ldots,\evec_m$ forms a basis for $\real^m$, which we call the *standard* basis for $\real^m$.

::::::

A basis for $\real^m$ forms a coordinate system for $\real^m$, as we will describe. Rather than continuing to write a list of vectors, we will find it convenient to denote a basis using a single symbol, such as 

$$
\begin{aligned}
\bcal = \{\vvec_1,\vvec_2,\ldots,\vvec_m\}
\end{aligned}
$$

:::::: example 例 3.2.2

In this section's preview activity, we considered the vectors 

$$
\begin{aligned}
\vvec_1 = \twovec{2}{1},
\vvec_2 = \twovec{1}{2}
\end{aligned}
$$

, which form a basis $\bcal=\{\vvec_1,\vvec_2\}$ for $\real^2$.

::: ula-sbs
In the standard coordinate system, the point $(2,-3)$ is found by moving 2 units to the right and 3 units down. We would like to define a new coordinate system where we interpret $(2,-3)$ to mean we move two times along $\vvec_1$ and 3 times along $-\vvec_2$. As we see in the figure, doing so leaves us at the point $(1,-4)$, expressed in the usual coordinate system.

![](/figures/ch3/pf-basis-1-2.svg)
:::

We have seen that 

$$
\begin{aligned}
\xvec = \twovec{1}{-4} = 2\vvec_1 - 3\vvec_2
\end{aligned}
$$

. The coordinates of the vector $\xvec$ in the new coordinate system are the weights that we use to create $\xvec$ as a linear combination of $\vvec_1$ and $\vvec_2$.

Since we now have two descriptions of the vector $\xvec$, we need some notation to keep track of which coordinate system we are using. Because $\twovec{1}{-4} = 2\vvec_1 - 3\vvec_2$, we will write 

$$
\begin{aligned}
\coords{\twovec{1}{-4}}{\bcal} =
\twovec{2}{-3}
\end{aligned}
$$

. More generally, $\coords{\xvec}{\bcal}$ will denote the coordinates of $\xvec$ in the basis $\bcal$; that is, $\coords{\xvec}{\bcal}$ is the vector $\twovec{c_1}{c_2}$ of weights such that 

$$
\begin{aligned}
\xvec = c_1\vvec_1 + c_2 \vvec_2
\end{aligned}
$$

.

For example, if the coordinates of $\xvec$ in the basis $\bcal$ are 

$$
\begin{aligned}
\coords{\xvec}{\bcal} = \twovec{5}{-2}
\end{aligned}
$$

, then 

$$
\begin{aligned}
\xvec = 5\vvec_1 - 2\vvec_2 = 5\twovec{2}{1}-2\twovec{1}{2}
= \twovec{8}{1},
\end{aligned}
$$

 and we conclude that 

$$
\begin{aligned}
\coords{\twovec{8}{1}}{\bcal} = \twovec{5}{-2}
\end{aligned}
$$

. This demonstrates how we can translate coordinates in the basis $\bcal$ into standard coordinates.

Suppose we know the expression of a vector $\xvec$ in standard coordinates. How can we find its coordinates in the basis $\bcal$? For instance, suppose $\xvec=\twovec{-8}{2}$ and that we would like to find $\coords{\xvec}{\bcal}$. We can write 

$$
\begin{aligned}
\coords{\twovec{-8}{2}}{\bcal}=\twovec{c_1}{c_2},
\end{aligned}
$$

 which means that 

$$
\begin{aligned}
\twovec{-8}{2} = c_1\vvec_1 + c_2\vvec_2
\end{aligned}
$$

 or 

$$
\begin{aligned}
c_1 \twovec{2}{1} + c_2 \twovec{1}{2} = \twovec{-8}{2}.
\end{aligned}
$$

 This linear system for the weights defines an augmented matrix 

$$
\left[\begin{array}{rr|r}
2 & 1 & -8 \\
1 & 2 & 2 \\
\end{array}\right]
\sim
\left[\begin{array}{rr|r}
1 & 0 & -6 \\
0 & 1 & 4 \\
\end{array}\right],
$$

. which means that 

$$
\begin{aligned}
\coords{\twovec{-8}{2}}{\bcal} = \twovec{-6}{4}
\end{aligned}
$$

.

::::::

This example illustrates how a basis in $\real^2$ provides a new coordinate system for $\real^2$ and shows how we may translate between this coordinate system and the standard one.

More generally, suppose that $\bcal=\{\vvec_1,\vvec_2,\ldots,\vvec_m\}$ is a basis for $\real^m$. We know that the span of the vectors is $\real^m$, which implies that any vector $\xvec$ in $\real^m$ can be written as a linear combination of the vectors. In addition, we know that the vectors are linearly independent, which means that we can write $\xvec$ as a linear combination of the vectors in exactly one way. Therefore, we have 

$$
\begin{aligned}
\xvec = c_1\vvec_1 + c_2\vvec_2 + \ldots + c_m\vvec_m
\end{aligned}
$$

 where the weights $c_1, c_2,\ldots, c_m$ are unique. In this case, we write the coordinate description of $\xvec$ in the basis $\bcal$ as 

$$
\begin{aligned}
\coords{\xvec}{\bcal} = \cfourvec{c_1}{c_2}{\vdots}{c_m}
\end{aligned}
$$

.

This activity demonstrates how we can efficiently convert between coordinate systems defined by different bases. Let's consider a basis $\bcal = \{\vvec_1,\vvec_2,\ldots,\vvec_m\}$ and a vector $\xvec$. We know that 

$$
\begin{aligned}
\xvec & {}={} c_1\vvec_1 +c_2\vvec_2+\ldots+c_m\vvec_m \\
\\
& {}={}
\left[\begin{array}{rrrr}
\vvec_1 & \vvec_2 & \cdots & \vvec_m
\end{array}\right]
\cfourvec{c_1}{c_2}{\vdots}{c_m} \\
\\
& {}={}
\left[\begin{array}{rrrr}
\vvec_1 & \vvec_2 & \cdots & \vvec_m
\end{array}\right]
\coords{\xvec}{\bcal}\text{.}
\end{aligned}
$$

 If we use $P_{\bcal}$ to denote the matrix whose columns are the basis vectors, then we find that 

$$
\begin{aligned}
\xvec = P_{\bcal}\coords{\xvec}{\bcal}
\end{aligned}
$$

 where 

$$
P_{\bcal} = \left[\begin{array}{rrrr}
\vvec_1 & \vvec_2 & \cdots & \vvec_m
\end{array}\right]
$$

. This means that the matrix $P_{\bcal}$ converts coordinates in the basis $\bcal$ into standard coordinates.

Since the columns of $P_{\bcal}$ are the basis vectors $\vvec_1,\vvec_2,\ldots,\vvec_m$, we know that $P_{\bcal}
\sim I_m$, and $P_{\bcal}$ is therefore invertible. Since we have 

$$
\begin{aligned}
\xvec = P_{\bcal}\coords{\xvec}{\bcal}
\end{aligned}
$$

, we must also have 

$$
\begin{aligned}
P_{\bcal}^{-1}\xvec = \coords{\xvec}{\bcal}
\end{aligned}
$$

.

<a id="proposition-coordinate-transform" class="ula-anchor"></a>

:::::: proposition 命题 3.2.2

If $\bcal$ is a basis and $P_{\bcal}$ the matrix whose columns are the basis vectors, then 

$$
\begin{aligned}
\xvec & = P_{\bcal}\coords{\xvec}{\bcal}, \\ \\
\coords{\xvec}{\bcal} & = P_{\bcal}^{-1}\xvec.
\end{aligned}
$$

::::::

If we have another basis $\ccal$, we find, in the same way, that $\xvec = P_{\ccal}\coords{\xvec}{\ccal}$ for the conversion between coordinates in the basis $\ccal$ into standard coordinates. We then have 

$$
\begin{aligned}
\coords{\xvec}{\bcal} = P^{-1}_{\bcal} \xvec
= P_{\bcal}^{-1}(P_{\ccal} \coords{\xvec}{\ccal})
= (P_{\bcal}^{-1}P_{\ccal}) \coords{\xvec}{\ccal}
\end{aligned}
$$

. Therefore, $P_{\bcal}^{-1}P_{\ccal}$ is the matrix that converts $\ccal$-coordinates into $\bcal$-coordinates.

Readers who are familiar with calculus may recognize that this change of basis converts a vector $\xvec$ into $\coords{\xvec}{\bcal}$, the set of changes in $\xvec$. This process is similar to differentiation in calculus. Similarly, the process of converting $\coords{\xvec}{\bcal}$ into the vector $\xvec$ adds together the changes in a process similar to integration. As a result, this change of basis represents a linear algebraic version of the Fundamental Theorem of Calculus.

We defined a basis to be a set of vectors $\bcal =
\{\vvec_1,\vvec_2,\ldots,\vvec_n\}$ that is linearly independent and whose span is $\real^m$. 

- A set of vectors forms a basis for $\real^m$ if and only if the matrix 
 
 $$
 A = \left[\begin{array}{rrrr}
 \vvec_1 & \vvec_2 & \cdots & \vvec_n
 \end{array}\right]
 \sim I
 $$
 
 . This means there must be $m$ vectors in a basis for $\real^m$.
- If $\vvec_1,\vvec_2,\ldots,\vvec_m$ forms a basis for $\real^m$, then any vector in $\real^m$ can be written as a linear combination of the vectors in exactly one way.
- We used the basis $\bcal$ to define a coordinate system in which $\coords{\xvec}{\bcal} = \cfourvec{c_1}{c_2}{\vdots}{c_n}$, the coordinates of $\xvec$ in the basis $\bcal$, are defined by 
 
 $$
 \begin{aligned}
 \xvec = c_1\vvec_1+c_2\vvec_2 + \cdots + c_n\vvec_m
 \end{aligned}
 $$
 
 .
- Forming the matrix $P_{\bcal}$ whose columns are the basis vectors, we can convert between coordinate systems: 
 
 $$
 \begin{aligned}
 x & {}={} P_{\bcal}\coords{\xvec}{\bcal}, \\
 P_{\bcal}^{-1} x & {}={} \coords{\xvec}{\bcal}. \\
 \end{aligned}
 $$

# 4.3 Diagonalization, similarity, and powers of a matrix

In [活动 4.1.3](/chapter-4/4-1#activity-eigen-intro), we explored a car rental company with two locations and known probabilities that a car rented at one location was returned to the same location. If the distribution of cars on a given day is recorded by the vector $\xvec_k$, we found a matrix equation $\xvec_{k+1}=A\xvec_k$ that determined how the distribution of cars changed from day to day.

To understand what happens over a number of days, we could rewrite this expression in terms of the initial distribution $\xvec_0$ as 

$$
\begin{aligned}
\xvec_k=A^k\xvec_0
\end{aligned}
$$

. Making long-term predictions then requires us to understand how large powers of the matrix $A$ behave. This section will introduce some ideas for determining this behavior.

The first example we considered in this chapter was the matrix 

$$
A=\left[\begin{array}{rr}
1 & 2 \\
2 & 1 \\
\end{array}\right]
$$

, which has eigenvectors $\vvec_1=\twovec{1}{1}$ and $\vvec_2 = \twovec{-1}{1}$ and associated eigenvalues $\lambda_1=3$ and $\lambda_2=-1$. In [The usefulness of eigenvalues and eigenvectors](/chapter-4/4-1#subsec-eigen-use), we described how $A$ is, in some sense, equivalent to the diagonal matrix 

$$
D = \left[\begin{array}{rr}
3 & 0 \\
0 & -1\\
\end{array}\right]
$$

.

This equivalence is summarized by [图 4.3.1](/chapter-4/4-3#fig-eigen-diag-A). The diagonal matrix $D$ has the geometric effect of stretching vectors horizontally by a factor of $3$ and flipping vectors vertically. The matrix $A$ has the geometric effect of stretching vectors by a factor of $3$ in the $\vvec_1$ direction and flipping them in the $\vvec_2$ direction. That is, the geometric effect of $A$ is the same as that of $D$ when viewed in a basis of eigenvectors of $A$.

More generally, suppose that we have an $n\times n$ matrix $A$ and that there is a basis of $\real^n$ consisting of eigenvectors $\vvec_1,\vvec_2,\ldots,\vvec_n$ of $A$ with associated eigenvalues $\lambda_1,\lambda_2,\ldots,\lambda_n$. If we use the eigenvectors to form the matrix 

$$
P = \begin{bmatrix}
\vvec_1 & \vvec_2 & \cdots & \vvec_n
\end{bmatrix}
$$

 and the eigenvalues to form the diagonal matrix 

$$
D = \left[\begin{array}{cccc}
\lambda_1 & 0 & \ldots & 0 \\
0 & \lambda_2 & \ldots & 0 \\
\vdots & \vdots & \ddots & 0 \\
0 & 0 & \ldots & \lambda_n \\
\end{array}\right]
$$

 and apply the same reasoning demonstrated in the activity, we find that $AP = PD$ and hence 

$$
\begin{aligned}
A=PDP^{-1}.
\end{aligned}
$$

We have now seen the following proposition.

<a id="prop-diagonalizable" class="ula-anchor"></a>

:::::: proposition 命题 4.3.1

If $A$ is an $n\times n$ matrix and there is a basis $\{\vvec_1,\vvec_2,\ldots,\vvec_n\}$ of $\real^n$ consisting of eigenvectors of $A$ having associated eigenvalues $\lambda_1, \lambda_2, \ldots, \lambda_n$, then we can write $A=PDP^{-1}$ where $D$ is the diagonal matrix whose diagonal entries are the eigenvalues of $A$ 

$$
D = \left[\begin{array}{cccc}
\lambda_1 & 0 & \ldots & 0 \\
0 & \lambda_2 & \ldots & 0 \\
\vdots & \vdots & \ddots & 0 \\
0 & 0 & \ldots & \lambda_n \\
\end{array}\right]
$$

 and the matrix 

$$
P = \left[\begin{array}{cccc}
\vvec_1 & \vvec_2 & \ldots & \vvec_n
\end{array}\right]
$$

.

::::::

<a id="4-3-example-1" class="ula-anchor"></a>

:::::: example 例 4.3.1

We have seen that 

$$
A = \begin{bmatrix}
1 & 2 \\
2 & 1 \\
\end{bmatrix}
$$

 has eigenvectors $\vvec_1 = \twovec11$ and $\vvec_2=\twovec{-1}1$ with associated eigenvalues $\lambda_1 = 3$ and $\lambda_2 = -1$. Forming the matrices 

$$
P = \begin{bmatrix}
\vvec_1 & \vvec_2
\end{bmatrix} =
\begin{bmatrix}
1 & -1 \\
1 & 1 \\
\end{bmatrix},~~~
D = \begin{bmatrix}
3 & 0 \\
0 & -1 \\
\end{bmatrix},
$$

 we see that $A=PDP^{-1}$.

This is the sense in which we mean that $A$ is equivalent to a diagonal matrix $D$. The expression $A=PDP^{-1}$ says that $A$, expressed in the basis defined by the columns of $P$, has the same geometric effect as $D$, expressed in the standard basis $\evec_1, \evec_2,\ldots,\evec_n$.

::::::

<a id="4-3-definition-1" class="ula-anchor"></a>

:::::: definition 定义 4.3.1

We say that the matrix $A$ is *diagonalizable* if there is a diagonal matrix $D$ and invertible matrix $P$ such that 

$$
\begin{aligned}
A = PDP^{-1}
\end{aligned}
$$

.

::::::

<a id="4-3-example-2" class="ula-anchor"></a>

:::::: example 例 4.3.2

We will try to find a diagonalization of 

$$
A =
\left[\begin{array}{rr}
-5 & 6 \\
-3 & 4 \\
\end{array}\right]
$$

 whose characteristic equation is 

$$
\begin{aligned}
\det(A-\lambda I) = (-5-\lambda)(4-\lambda)+18 =
(-2-\lambda)(1-\lambda) = 0
\end{aligned}
$$

. This shows that the eigenvalues of $A$ are $\lambda_1 =
-2$ and $\lambda_2 = 1$.

By constructing $\nul(A-(-2)I)$, we find a basis for $E_{-2}$ consisting of the vector $\vvec_1 =
\twovec{2}{1}$. Similarly, a basis for $E_1$ consists of the vector $\vvec_2 = \twovec{1}{1}$. This shows that we can construct a basis $\{\vvec_1,\vvec_2\}$ of $\real^2$ consisting of eigenvectors of $A$.

We now form the matrices 

$$
D = \left[\begin{array}{rr}
-2 & 0 \\
0 & 1 \\
\end{array}\right],\qquad
P = \left[\begin{array}{cc} \vvec_1 & \vvec_2
\end{array}\right] =
\left[\begin{array}{rr}
2 & 1 \\
1 & 1 \\
\end{array}\right]
$$

 and verify that 

$$
PDP^{-1} =
\left[\begin{array}{rr}
2 & 1 \\
1 & 1 \\
\end{array}\right]
\left[\begin{array}{rr}
-2 & 0 \\
0 & 1 \\
\end{array}\right]
\left[\begin{array}{rr}
1 & -1 \\
-1 & 2 \\
\end{array}\right]
=
\left[\begin{array}{rr}
-5 & 6 \\
-3 & 4 \\
\end{array}\right] = A
$$

.

There are, in fact, many ways to diagonalize $A$. For instance, we could change the order of the eigenvalues and eigenvectors and write 

$$
D = \left[\begin{array}{rr}
1 & 0 \\
0 & -2 \\
\end{array}\right],\qquad
P = \left[\begin{array}{cc} \vvec_2 & \vvec_1
\end{array}\right] =
\left[\begin{array}{rr}
1 & 2 \\
1 & 1 \\
\end{array}\right]
$$

.

If we choose a different basis for the eigenspaces, we will also find a different matrix $P$ that diagonalizes $A$. The point is that there are many ways in which $A$ can be written in the form $A=PDP^{-1}$.

::::::

<a id="4-3-example-3" class="ula-anchor"></a>

:::::: example 例 4.3.3

We will try to find a diagonalization of 

$$
A =
\left[\begin{array}{rr}
0 & 4 \\
-1 & 4 \\
\end{array}\right]
$$

.

Once again, we find the eigenvalues by solving the characteristic equation: 

$$
\begin{aligned}
\det(A-\lambda I) = -\lambda(4-\lambda) + 4 = (2-\lambda)^2
= 0
\end{aligned}
$$

. In this case, there is a single eigenvalue $\lambda=2$.

We find a basis for the eigenspace $E_2$ by describing $\nul(A-2I)$: 

$$
A-2I = \left[\begin{array}{rr}
-2 & 4 \\
-1 & 2 \\
\end{array}\right]
\sim
\left[\begin{array}{rr}
1 & -2 \\
0 & 0 \\
\end{array}\right]
$$

. This shows that the eigenspace $E_2$ is one-dimensional with $\vvec_1=\twovec{2}{1}$ forming a basis.

In this case, there is not a basis of $\real^2$ consisting of eigenvectors of $A$, which tells us that $A$ is not diagonalizable.

::::::

In fact, if we only know that $A = PDP^{-1}$, we can say that the columns of $P$ are eigenvectors of $A$ and that the diagonal entries of $D$ are the associated eigenvalues.

<a id="prop-diagonalizability" class="ula-anchor"></a>

:::::: proposition 命题 4.3.2

An $n\times n$ matrix $A$ is diagonalizable if and only if there is a basis of $\real^n$ consisting of eigenvectors of $A$.

::::::

<a id="prop-powers-diag" class="ula-anchor"></a>

:::::: proposition 命题 4.3.3

If $A=PDP^{-1}$, then $A^k = PD^kP^{-1}$. When $A$ is invertible, we also have $A^{-1} =
PD^{-1}P^{-1}$.

\end{bmatrix}
$$

 and the initial vector $\xvec_0 = \ctwovec{1000}0$. We were interested in understanding the sequence of vectors $\xvec_{k+1} = A\xvec_k$, which means that $\xvec_k =
A^k\xvec_0$.

We can verify that $\vvec_1 = \twovec31$ and $\vvec_2 =
\twovec{-1}1$ are eigenvectors of $A$ having associated eigenvalues $\lambda_1=1$ and $\lambda_2 =

Notice that 

$$

\end{bmatrix}

\end{bmatrix}
$$

. As $k$ increases, $0.2^k$ becomes closer and closer to zero. This means that for very large powers $k$, we have 

$$

\end{bmatrix}

$$

\end{bmatrix}P^{-1} =

\end{bmatrix}.

We have been interested in diagonalizing a matrix $A$ because doing so relates a matrix $A$ to a simpler diagonal matrix $D$. In particular, the effect of multiplying a vector by $A=PDP^{-1}$, viewed in the basis defined by the columns of $P$, is the same as the effect of multiplying by $D$ in the standard basis.

While many matrices are diagonalizable, there are some that are not. For example, if a matrix has complex eigenvalues, it is not possible to find a basis of $\real^n$ consisting of eigenvectors, which means that the matrix is not diagonalizable. In this case, however, we can still relate the matrix to a simpler form that explains the geometric effect this matrix has on vectors.

<a id="4-3-definition-2" class="ula-anchor"></a>

:::::: definition 定义 4.3.2

We say that $A$ is *similar* to $B$ if there is an invertible matrix $P$ such that $A = PBP^{-1}$.

::::::

Notice that a matrix is diagonalizable if and only if it is similar to a diagonal matrix. In case a matrix $A$ has complex eigenvalues, we will find a simpler matrix $C$ that is similar to $A$ and note that $A=PCP^{-1}$ has the same effect, when viewed in the basis defined by the columns of $P$, as $C$, when viewed in the standard basis.

$$

$$

.

::::::

If the $2\times2$ matrix $A$ has a complex eigenvalue $\lambda = a + bi$, it turns out that $A$ is always similar to the matrix 

$$
C = \left[\begin{array}{rr}
a & -b \\
b & a \\
\end{array}\right],
$$

 whose geometric effect on vectors can be described in terms of a rotation and a scaling. There is, in fact, a method for finding the matrix $P$ so that $A=PCP^{-1}$ that we'll see in exercise-complex-eigenvector. For now, we note that $A$ has the same geometric effect as $C$, when viewed in the basis provided by the columns of $P$. We will put this fact to use in the next section to understand certain dynamical systems.

<a id="4-3-proposition-4" class="ula-anchor"></a>

:::::: proposition 命题 4.3.4

If $A$ is a $2\times2$ matrix with a complex eigenvalue $\lambda = a + bi$, then $A$ is similar to 

$$
C = \begin{bmatrix}
a & -b \\
b & a \\
\end{bmatrix}
$$

; that is, there is a matrix $P$ such that $A= PCP^{-1}$.

::::::

Our goal in this section has been to use the eigenvalues and eigenvectors of a matrix $A$ to relate $A$ to a simpler matrix. 

- We said that $A$ is diagonalizable if we can write $A = PDP^{-1}$ where $D$ is a diagonal matrix. The columns of $P$ consist of eigenvectors of $A$ and the diagonal entries of $D$ are the associated eigenvalues.
- An $n\times n$ matrix $A$ is diagonalizable if and only if there is a basis of $\real^n$ consisting of eigenvectors of $A$.
- We said that $A$ and $B$ are similar if there is an invertible matrix $P$ such that $A=PBP^{-1}$. In this case, $A^k = PB^kP^{-1}$.
- If $A$ is a $2\times2$ matrix with complex eigenvalue $\lambda = a+bi$, then $A$ is similar to 
 
 $$
 C = \left[\begin{array}{rr}
 a & -b \\
 b & a \\
 \end{array} \right]
 $$
 
 . Writing the point $(a,b)$ in polar coordinates $r$ and $\theta$, we see that $C$ rotates vectors through an angle $\theta$ and scales them by a factor of $r=\sqrt{a^2+b^2}$.

$$

$$

\det(A)=\det(PBP^{-1})

\end{aligned}

\end{aligned}
$$

\end{aligned}

\end{aligned}

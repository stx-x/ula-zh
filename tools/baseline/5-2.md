# 5.2 Finding eigenvectors numerically

We have typically found eigenvalues of a square matrix $A$ as the roots of the characteristic polynomial $\det(A-\lambda I) = 0$ and the associated eigenvectors as the null space $\nul(A-\lambda I)$. Unfortunately, this approach is not practical when we are working with large matrices. First, finding the charactertic polynomial of a large matrix requires considerable computation, as does finding the roots of that polynomial. Second, finding the null space of a singular matrix is plagued by numerical problems, as we will see in the preview activity.

For this reason, we will explore a technique called the *power method* that finds numerical approximations to the eigenvalues and eigenvectors of a square matrix.

<a id="ula-preview-5-2" class="ula-anchor"></a>

:::::: activity 活动 5.2.1

Let's recall some earlier observations about eigenvalues and eigenvectors.

**（a）** How are the eigenvalues and associated eigenvectors of $A$ related to those of $A^{-1}$?

::::: details 查看解答

If $\lambda$ is an eigenvalue of $A$, then $A\vvec=\lambda\vvec$ for an associated eigenvector $\vvec$. Multiplying by $A^{-1}$ and $\lambda^{-1}$, we obtain $\lambda^{-1}\vvec =
A^{-1}\vvec$, which shows that $\lambda^{-1}$ is an eigenvalue of $A^{-1}$.

:::::

**（b）** How are the eigenvalues and associated eigenvectors of $A$ related to those of $A-3I$?

::::: details 查看解答

In the same way, if $\lambda$ is an eigenvalue of $A$, then $A\vvec=\lambda\vvec$ for an associated eigenvector $\vvec$. This means that $(A-3I)\vvec = (\lambda-3)\vvec$ so that $\lambda-3$ is an eigenvalue of $A-3I$.

:::::

**（c）** If $\lambda$ is an eigenvalue of $A$, what can we say about the pivot positions of $A-\lambda
I$?

::::: details 查看解答

If $\lambda$ is an eigenvalue of $A$, then $A-\lambda I$ is not invertible and so has a row without a pivot position.

:::::

:::::

**（e）** Suppose that $\xvec_0 = \twovec{1}{0}$, and we define a sequence $\xvec_{k+1} = A\xvec_k$; in other words, $\xvec_{k} = A^k\xvec_0$. What happens to $\xvec_k$ as $k$ grows increasingly large?

::::: details 查看解答

The vectors $\xvec_k$ form a Markov chain, which must converge to the steady-state vector $\qvec=\twovec{\frac23}{\frac13}$.

:::::

**（f）** Explain how the eigenvalues of $A$ are responsible for the behavior noted in the previous question.

::::: details 查看解答

We have eigenvalues $\lambda_1=1$ and $\lambda_2=0.4$. If we begin with $\xvec_0=c_1\vvec_1+c_2\vvec_2$ and successively multiply by $A$, we have $\xvec_k=c_1\vvec_1 + c_2(0.4)^k\vvec_2$. When $k$ becomes large, the coefficient of $\vvec_2$ becomes insignificantly small so we are left with an eigenvector in $E_1$.

Our goal is to find a technique that produces numerical approximations to the eigenvalues and associated eigenvectors of a matrix $A$. We begin by searching for the eigenvalue having the largest absolute value, which is called the *dominant* eigenvalue. The next two examples demonstrate this technique.

<a id="5-2-example-1" class="ula-anchor"></a>

:::::: example 例 5.2.1

Let's begin with the positive stochastic matrix 

$$
A=\left[\begin{array}{rr}
0.7 & 0.6 \\
0.3 & 0.4 \\
\end{array}\right]
$$

. We spent quite a bit of time studying this type of matrix in 第 4.5 节; in particular, we saw that any Markov chain will converge to the unique steady-state vector. Let's rephrase this statement in terms of the eigenvectors of $A$.

This matrix has eigenvalues $\lambda_1 = 1$ and $\lambda_2 =0.1$ so the dominant eigenvalue is $\lambda_1 = 1$. The associated eigenvectors are $\vvec_1 =
\twovec{2}{1}$ and $\vvec_2 = \twovec{-1}{1}$. Suppose we begin with the vector 

$$
\begin{aligned}
\xvec_0 = \twovec{1}{0} = \frac13 \vvec_1 - \frac13 \vvec_2
\end{aligned}
$$

 and find 

$$
\begin{aligned}
\xvec_1 & {}={} A\xvec_0 = \frac13 \vvec_1 - \frac13(0.1)
\vvec_2 \\
\xvec_2 & {}={} A^2\xvec_0 = \frac13 \vvec_1 - \frac13(0.1)^2
\vvec_2 \\
\xvec_3 & {}={} A^3\xvec_0 = \frac13 \vvec_1 - \frac13(0.1)^3
\vvec_2 \\
& \vdots \\
\xvec_k & {}={} A^k\xvec_0 = \frac13 \vvec_1 - \frac13(0.1)^k
\vvec_2 \\
\end{aligned}
$$

 and so forth. Notice that the powers $0.1^k$ become increasingly small as $k$ grows so that $\xvec_k\approx
\frac13\vvec_1$ when $k$ is large. Therefore, the vectors $\xvec_k$ become increasingly close to a vector in the eigenspace $E_1$, the eigenspace associated to the dominant eigenvalue. If we did not know the eigenvector $\vvec_1$, we could use a Markov chain in this way to find a basis vector for $E_1$, which, as seen in 第 4.5 节, is essentially how the Google PageRank algorithm works.

::::::

<a id="5-2-example-2" class="ula-anchor"></a>

:::::: example 例 5.2.2

Let's now look at the matrix 

$$
A = \left[\begin{array}{rr}
2 & 1 \\ 1 & 2 \\ \end{array}\right]
$$

, which has eigenvalues $\lambda_1=3$ and $\lambda_2 = 1$. The dominant eigenvalue is $\lambda_1=3$, and the associated eigenvectors are $\vvec_1 = \twovec{1}{1}$ and $\vvec_{2} =
\twovec{-1}{1}$. Once again, begin with the vector $\xvec_0 = \twovec{1}{0}=\frac12 \vvec_1 - \frac12 \vvec_2$ so that 

$$
\begin{aligned}
\xvec_1 & {}={} A\xvec_0 = 3\frac12 \vvec_1 - \frac12
\vvec_2 \\
\xvec_2 & {}={} A^2\xvec_0 = 3^2\frac13 \vvec_1 - \frac12
\vvec_2 \\
\xvec_3 & {}={} A^3\xvec_0 = 3^3\frac13 \vvec_1 - \frac12
\vvec_2 \\
& \vdots \\
\xvec_k & {}={} A^k\xvec_0 = 3^k\frac13 \vvec_1 - \frac12
\vvec_2\text{.} \\
\end{aligned}
$$

::: ula-sbs
As the figure shows, the vectors $\xvec_k$ are stretched by a factor of $3$ in the $\vvec_1$ direction and not at all in the $\vvec_2$ direction. Consequently, the vectors $\xvec_k$ become increasingly long, but their direction becomes closer to the direction of the eigenvector $\vvec_1=\twovec{1}{1}$ associated to the dominant eigenvalue.

![](/figures/ch5/pf-numerical-power.svg)
:::

To find an eigenvector associated to the dominant eigenvalue, we will prevent the length of the vectors $\xvec_k$ from growing arbitrarily large by multiplying by an appropriate scaling constant. Here is one way to do this. Given the vector $\xvec_k$, we identify its component having the largest absolute value and call it $m_k$. We then define $\overline{\xvec}_k = \frac{1}{m_k} \xvec_k$, which means that the component of $\overline{\xvec}_k$ having the largest absolute value is $1$.

For example, beginning with $\xvec_0 = \twovec{1}{0}$, we find $\xvec_1 = A\xvec_{0} =
\twovec{2}{1}$. The component of $\xvec_1$ having the largest absolute value is $m_1=2$ so we multiply by $\frac{1}{m_1} = \frac12$ to obtain $\overline{\xvec}_1 =
\twovec{1}{\frac12}$. Then $\xvec_2 = A\overline{\xvec}_1 =
\twovec{\frac52}{2}$. Now the component having the largest absolute value is $m_2=\frac52$ so we multiply by $\frac25$ to obtain $\overline{\xvec}_2 = \twovec{1}{\frac45}$.

::: ula-sbs
The resulting sequence of vectors $\overline{\xvec}_k$ is shown in the figure. Notice how the vectors $\overline{\xvec}_k$ now approach the eigenvector $\vvec_1$, which gives us a way to find the eigenvector $\vvec=\twovec{1}{1}$. This is the *power method* for finding an eigenvector associated to the dominant eigenvalue of a matrix.

![](/figures/ch5/pf-numerical-power-norm.svg)
:::

Notice that the power method gives us not only an eigenvector $\vvec$ but also its associated eigenvalue. As in the activity, consider the matrix 

$$
A=\left[\begin{array}{rr}
-5.1 & 5.7 \\
-3.8 & 4.4 \\
\end{array}\right]
$$

, which has eigenvector $\vvec=\twovec{3}{2}$. The first component has the largest absolute value so we multiply by $\frac13$ to obtain $\overline{\vvec}=\twovec{1}{\frac23}$. When we multiply by $A$, we have $A\overline{\vvec} = \twovec{-1.30}{-0.86}$. Notice that the first component still has the largest absolute value so that the multiplier $m=-1.3$ is the eigenvalue $\lambda$ corresponding to the eigenvector. This demonstrates the fact that the multipliers $m_k$ approach the eigenvalue $\lambda$ having the largest absolute value.

Notice that the power method requires us to choose an initial vector $\xvec_0$. For most choices, this method will find the eigenvalue having the largest absolute value. However, an unfortunate choice of $\xvec_0$ may not. For instance, if we had chosen $\xvec_0 = \vvec_2$ in our example above, the vectors in the sequence $\xvec_k =
A^k\xvec_0=\lambda_2^k\vvec_2$ will not detect the eigenvector $\vvec_1$. However, it usually happens that our initial guess $\xvec_0$ has some contribution from $\vvec_1$ that enables us to find it.

The power method, as presented here, will fail for certain unlucky matrices. This is examined in exercise-power-method along with a means to improve the power method to work for all matrices.

The power method gives a technique for finding the dominant eigenvalue of a matrix. We can modify the method to find the other eigenvalues as well.

There are some restrictions on the matrices to which this technique applies as we have assumed that the eigenvalues of $A$ are real and distinct. If $A$ has repeated or complex eigenvalues, this technique will need to be modified, as explored in some of the exercises.

We have explored the power method as a tool for numerically approximating the eigenvalues and eigenvectors of a matrix. 

- After choosing an initial vector $\xvec_0$, we define the sequence $\xvec_{k+1}=A\xvec_k$. As $k$ grows larger, the direction of the vectors $\xvec_k$ closely approximates the direction of the eigenspace corresponding to the eigenvalue $\lambda_1$ having the largest absolute value.
- We normalize the vectors $\xvec_k$ by multiplying by $\frac{1}{m_k}$, where $m_k$ is the component having the largest absolute value. In this way, the vectors $\overline{\xvec}_k$ approach an eigenvector associated to $\lambda_1$, and the multipliers $m_k$ approach the eigenvalue $\lambda_1$.
- To find the eigenvalue having the smallest absolute value, we apply the power method using the matrix $A^{-1}$.
- To find the eigenvalue closest to some number $s$, we apply the power method using the matrix $(A-sI)^{-1}$.

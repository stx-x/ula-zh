# 6.4 Finding orthogonal bases

The last section demonstrated the value of working with orthogonal, and especially orthonormal, sets. If we have an orthogonal basis $\wvec_1,\wvec_2,\ldots,\wvec_n$ for a subspace $W$, the [命题 6.3.4](/chapter-6/6-3#prop-proj-formula) tells us that the orthogonal projection of a vector $\bvec$ onto $W$ is 

$$
\begin{aligned}
\bhat =
\frac{\bvec\cdot\wvec_1}{\wvec_1\cdot\wvec_1}~\wvec_1 +
\frac{\bvec\cdot\wvec_2}{\wvec_2\cdot\wvec_2}~\wvec_2 +
\cdots +
\frac{\bvec\cdot\wvec_n}{\wvec_n\cdot\wvec_n}~\wvec_n\text{.}
\end{aligned}
$$

 An orthonormal basis $\uvec_1,\uvec_2,\ldots,\uvec_n$ is even more convenient: after forming the matrix 

$$
Q=\begin{bmatrix} \uvec_1 & \uvec_2 & \ldots &
\uvec_n
\end{bmatrix}
$$

, we have $\bhat = QQ^T\bvec$.

In the examples we've seen so far, however, orthogonal bases were given to us. What we need now is a way to form orthogonal bases. In this section, we'll explore an algorithm that begins with a basis for a subspace and creates an orthogonal basis. Once we have an orthogonal basis, we can scale each of the vectors appropriately to produce an orthonormal basis.

As this activity illustrates, Gram-Schmidt orthogonalization begins with a basis $\vvec_1\vvec_2,\ldots,\vvec_n$ for a subspace $W$ of $\real^m$ and creates an orthogonal basis for $W$. Let's work through a second example.

<a id="example-gram-schmidt" class="ula-anchor"></a>

:::::: example 例 6.4.1

Let's start with the basis 

$$
\begin{aligned}
\vvec_1=\threevec{2}{-1}2,\hspace{24pt}
\vvec_2=\threevec{-3}{3}0,\hspace{24pt}
\vvec_3=\threevec{-2}71\text{,}
\end{aligned}
$$

 which is a basis for $\real^3$.

To get started, we'll simply set $\wvec_1=\vvec_1=\threevec{2}{-1}2$. We construct $\wvec_2$ from $\vvec_2$ by subtracting its orthogonal projection onto $W_1$, the line defined by $\wvec_1$. This gives 

$$
\begin{aligned}
\wvec_2 = \vvec_2 -
\frac{\vvec_2\cdot\wvec_1}{\wvec_1\cdot\wvec_1}\wvec_1 =
\vvec_2 + \wvec_1 = \threevec{-1}22\text{.}
\end{aligned}
$$

Notice that we found $\vvec_2 = -\wvec_1 + \wvec_2$. Therefore, we can rewrite any linear combination of $\vvec_1$ and $\vvec_2$ as 

$$
\begin{aligned}
c_1\vvec_1 + c_2\vvec_2 = c_1\wvec_1 + c_2(-\wvec_1+\wvec_2)
= (c_1-c_2)\wvec_1 + c_2\wvec_2\text{,}
\end{aligned}
$$

 a linear combination of $\wvec_1$ and $\wvec_2$. This tells us that 

$$
\begin{aligned}
W_2 = \laspan{\wvec_1,\wvec_2} =
\laspan{\vvec_1,\vvec_2}\text{.}
\end{aligned}
$$

 In other words, $\wvec_1$ and $\wvec_2$ is a orthogonal basis for $W_2$, the 2-dimensional subspace that is the span of $\vvec_1$ and $\vvec_2$.

Finally, we form $\wvec_3$ from $\vvec_3$ by subtracting its orthogonal projection onto $W_2$: 

$$
\begin{aligned}
\wvec_3 = \vvec_3 -
\frac{\vvec_3\cdot\wvec_1}{\wvec_1\cdot\wvec_1}\wvec_1 -
\frac{\vvec_3\cdot\wvec_2}{\wvec_2\cdot\wvec_2}\wvec_2
= \vvec_3 + \wvec_1 - 2\wvec_2
= \threevec22{-1}\text{.}
\end{aligned}
$$

We can now check that 

$$
\begin{aligned}
\wvec_1=\threevec2{-1}2,\hspace{24pt}
\wvec_2=\threevec{-1}22,\hspace{24pt}
\wvec_3=\threevec22{-1},\hspace{24pt}
\end{aligned}
$$

 is an orthogonal set. Furthermore, we have, as before, $\laspan{\wvec_1,\wvec_2,\wvec_3}
= \laspan{\vvec_1,\vvec_2,\vvec_3}$, which says that we have found a new orthogonal basis for $\real^3$.

To create an orthonormal basis, we form unit vectors parallel to each of the vectors in the orthogonal basis: 

$$
\begin{aligned}
\uvec_1 = \threevec{2/3}{-1/3}{2/3},\hspace{24pt}
\uvec_2 = \threevec{-1/3}{2/3}{2/3},\hspace{24pt}
\uvec_3 = \threevec{2/3}{2/3}{-1/3}\text{.}
\end{aligned}
$$

::::::

More generally, if we have a basis $\vvec_1,\vvec_2,\ldots,\vvec_n$ for a subspace $W$ of $\real^m$, the Gram-Schmidt algorithm creates an orthogonal basis for $W$ in the following way: 

$$
\begin{aligned}
\wvec_1 & = \vvec_1


\wvec_2 & = \vvec_2 -
\frac{\vvec_2\cdot\wvec_1}{\wvec_1\cdot\wvec_1}\wvec_1


\wvec_3 & = \vvec_3 -
\frac{\vvec_3\cdot\wvec_1}{\wvec_1\cdot\wvec_1}\wvec_1 -
\frac{\vvec_3\cdot\wvec_2}{\wvec_2\cdot\wvec_2}\wvec_2


& \vdots


\wvec_n & = \vvec_n -
\frac{\vvec_n\cdot\wvec_1}{\wvec_1\cdot\wvec_1}\wvec_1 -
\frac{\vvec_n\cdot\wvec_2}{\wvec_2\cdot\wvec_2}\wvec_2 -
\ldots -
\frac{\vvec_n\cdot\wvec_{n-1}}
{\wvec_{n-1}\cdot\wvec_{n-1}}\wvec_{n-1}
\text{.}
\end{aligned}
$$

From here, we may form an orthonormal basis by constructing a unit vector parallel to each vector in the orthogonal basis: $\uvec_j = 1/\len{\wvec_j}~\wvec_j$.

When the columns of a matrix $A$ are linearly independent, they form a basis for $\col(A)$ so that we can perform the Gram-Schmidt algorithm. The previous activity shows how this leads to a factorization of $A$ as the product of a matrix $Q$ whose columns are an orthonormal basis for $\col(A)$ and an upper triangular matrix $R$.

<a id="prop-qr" class="ula-anchor"></a>

:::::: proposition 命题 6.4.1 · $QR$ factorization

If $A$ is an $m\times n$ matrix whose columns are linearly independent, we may write $A=QR$ where $Q$ is an $m\times n$ matrix whose columns form an orthonormal basis for $\col(A)$ and $R$ is an $n\times n$ upper triangular matrix.

::::::

<a id="6-4-example-2" class="ula-anchor"></a>

:::::: example 例 6.4.2

We'll consider the matrix 

$$
A=\begin{bmatrix}
2 & -3 & -2 \\
-1 & 3 & 7 \\
2 & 0 & 1 \\
\end{bmatrix}
$$

 whose columns, which we'll denote $\vvec_1$, $\vvec_2$, and $\vvec_3$, are the basis of $\real^3$ that we considered in [例 6.4.1](/chapter-6/6-4#example-gram-schmidt). There we found an orthogonal basis $\wvec_1$, $\wvec_2$, and $\wvec_3$ that satisfied 

$$
\begin{aligned}
\vvec_1 & {}={} \wvec_1


\vvec_2 & {}={} -\wvec_1 + \wvec_2


\vvec_3 & {}={} -\wvec_1 + 2\wvec_2 + \wvec _3\text{.}
\end{aligned}
$$

In terms of the resulting orthonormal basis $\uvec_1$, $\uvec_2$, and $\uvec_3$, we had 

$$
\begin{aligned}
\wvec_1 = 3 \uvec_1,\hspace{24pt}
\wvec_2 = 3 \uvec_2,\hspace{24pt}
\wvec_3 = 3 \uvec_3
\end{aligned}
$$

 so that 

$$
\begin{aligned}
\vvec_1 & {}={} 3\uvec_1


\vvec_2 & {}={} -3\uvec_1 + 3\uvec_2


\vvec_3 & {}={} -3\uvec_1 + 6\uvec_2 + 3\uvec _3\text{.}
\end{aligned}
$$

Therefore, if 

$$
Q=\begin{bmatrix} \uvec_1 & \uvec_2 &
\uvec_3 \end{bmatrix}
$$

, we have the $QR$ factorization 

$$
A = Q\begin{bmatrix}
3 & -3 & -3 \\
0 & 3 & 6 \\
0 & 0 & 3 \\
\end{bmatrix}
=QR\text{.}
$$

::::::

In fact, Sage provides its own version of the $QR$ factorization that is a bit different than the way we've developed the factorization here. For this reason, we have provided our own version of the factorization.

This section explored the Gram-Schmidt orthogonalization algorithm and how it leads to the matrix factorization $A=QR$ when the columns of $A$ are linearly independent. 

- Beginning with a basis $\vvec_1,
 \vvec_2,\ldots,\vvec_n$ for a subspace $W$ of $\real^m$, the vectors 
 
 $$
 \begin{aligned}
 \wvec_1 & = \vvec_1
 
 
 \wvec_2 & = \vvec_2 -
 \frac{\vvec_2\cdot\wvec_1}{\wvec_1\cdot\wvec_1}\wvec_1
 
 
 \wvec_3 & = \vvec_3 -
 \frac{\vvec_3\cdot\wvec_1}{\wvec_1\cdot\wvec_1}\wvec_1 -
 \frac{\vvec_3\cdot\wvec_2}{\wvec_2\cdot\wvec_2}\wvec_2
 
 
 & \vdots
 
 
 \wvec_n & = \vvec_n -
 \frac{\vvec_n\cdot\wvec_1}{\wvec_1\cdot\wvec_1}\wvec_1 -
 \frac{\vvec_n\cdot\wvec_2}{\wvec_2\cdot\wvec_2}\wvec_2 -
 \ldots -
 \frac{\vvec_n\cdot\wvec_{n-1}}
 {\wvec_{n-1}\cdot\wvec_{n-1}}\wvec_{n-1}
 \end{aligned}
 $$
 
 form an orthogonal basis for $W$.
- We may scale each vector $\wvec_i$ appropriately to obtain an orthonormal basis $\uvec_1,\uvec_2,\ldots,\uvec_n$.
- Expressing the Gram-Schmidt algorithm in matrix form shows that, if the columns of $A$ are linearly independent, then we can write $A=QR$, where the columns of $Q$ form an orthonormal basis for $\col(A)$ and $R$ is upper triangular.

$$
\begin{aligned}
Set \wvec_1 = \vvec_1.
\end{aligned}
$$

$$
\begin{aligned}
Find the orthogonal projection
\vhat_2=\frac{\vvec_2\cdot \wvec_1}{\wvec_1\cdot\wvec_1}\wvec_1
of  \vvec_2 onto \laspan{\wvec_1}.
\end{aligned}
$$

$$
\begin{aligned}
Compute \wvec_2 = \vvec_2 - \vhat_2.
\end{aligned}
$$

$$
\begin{aligned}
Find the orthogonal projection
\vhat_3=\frac{\vvec_3\cdot \wvec_1}{\wvec_1\cdot\wvec_1}\wvec_1+\frac{\vvec_3\cdot \wvec_2}{\wvec_2\cdot\wvec_2}\wvec_2
of  \vvec_3 onto \laspan{\wvec_1,\wvec_2}.
\end{aligned}
$$

$$
\begin{aligned}
Compute \wvec_3 = \vvec_3 - \vhat_3.
\end{aligned}
$$

$$
\begin{aligned}
Scale each of the vectors \wvec_1,
\wvec_2, and \wvec_3
to give an orthonormal basis
\{\uvec_1,\uvec_2,\uvec_3\}
of \real^3.
\end{aligned}
$$

$$
\begin{aligned}
Scale each of the vectors \vvec_1,
\vvec_2, and \vvec_3
to give an orthonormal basis
\{\uvec_1,\uvec_2,\uvec_3\}
of \real^3.
\end{aligned}
$$

$$
\begin{aligned}
Find the orthogonal projection
\vhat_3=\frac{\vvec_3\cdot \vvec_1}{\vvec_1\cdot\vvec_1}\vvec_1+\frac{\vvec_3\cdot \vvec_2}{\vvec_2\cdot\vvec_2}\vvec_2

\begin{aligned}

# 6.1 The dot product

Although we originally introduced vectors as a way to gain geometric insight into systems of linear equations, we have yet to leverage all the geometric information available from this perspective. In this section, we introduce a simple algebraic operation, known as the *dot product*, that helps us measure the length of vectors and the angle formed by a pair of vectors.

For two-dimensional vectors $\vvec$ and $\wvec$, their dot product $\vvec\cdot\wvec$ is the scalar defined to be 

$$
\begin{aligned}
\vvec\cdot\wvec = \twovec{v_1}{v_2}\cdot\twovec{w_1}{w_2} =
v_1w_1 + v_2w_2\text{.}
\end{aligned}
$$

 For instance, 

$$
\begin{aligned}
\twovec{2}{-3}\cdot\twovec{4}{1} = 2\cdot 4 + (-3)\cdot 1 = 5.
\end{aligned}
$$

The dot product is defined, more generally, for any two $m$-dimensional vectors: 

$$
\vvec\cdot\wvec =
\left[
\begin{array}{c}
v_1 \\ v_2 \\ \vdots \\ v_m \\
\end{array}
\right]
\cdot
\left[
\begin{array}{c}
w_1 \\ w_2 \\ \vdots \\ w_m \\
\end{array}
\right]
= v_1w_1 + v_2w_2 + \ldots + v_mw_m\text{.}
$$

 The important thing to remember is that the dot product will produce a scalar. In other words, the two vectors are combined in such a way as to create a number, and, as we'll see, this number conveys useful geometric information.

<a id="6-1-example-1" class="ula-anchor"></a>

:::::: example 例 6.1.1

We compute the dot product between two four-dimensional vectors as 

$$
\left[
\begin{array}{c}
2 \\ 0 \\ -3 \\ 1 \\
\end{array}
\right]
\cdot
\left[
\begin{array}{c}
-1 \\ 3 \\ 1 \\ 2 \\
\end{array}
\right]
= 2(-1) + 0(3) + (-3)(1) + 1(2) = -3\text{.}
$$

::::::

:::::: note Properties of dot products

As with ordinary multiplication, the dot product enjoys some familiar algebraic properties, such as commutativity and distributivity. More specifically, it doesn't matter in which order we compute the dot product of two vectors: 

$$
\begin{aligned}
\vvec\cdot\wvec = \wvec\cdot\vvec\text{.}
\end{aligned}
$$

 If $s$ is a scalar, we have 

$$
\begin{aligned}
(s\vvec)\cdot\wvec = s(\vvec\cdot\wvec)\text{.}
\end{aligned}
$$

 We may also distribute the dot product across linear combinations: 

$$
\begin{aligned}
(c_1\vvec_1+c_2\vvec_2)\cdot\wvec = c_1\vvec_1\cdot\wvec +
c_2\vvec_2\cdot\wvec\text{.}
\end{aligned}
$$

::::::

<a id="6-1-example-2" class="ula-anchor"></a>

:::::: example 例 6.1.2

Suppose that $\vvec_1\cdot\wvec = 4$ and $\vvec_2\cdot\wvec = -7$. Then 

$$
\begin{aligned}
(2\vvec_1)\cdot\wvec & {}={} 2(\vvec_1\cdot\wvec) =
2(4) = 8 \\
(-3\vvec_1+ 2\vvec_2)\cdot\wvec & {}={}
-3(\vvec_1\cdot\wvec) + 2(\vvec_2\cdot\wvec) = -3(4)+2(-7) =
-26\text{.}
\end{aligned}
$$

::::::

The most important property of the dot product, and the real reason for our interest in it, is that it gives us geometric information about vectors and their relationship to one another. Let's first think about the length of a vector by looking at the vector $\vvec = \twovec32$ as shown in [图 6.1.1](/chapter-6/6-1#fig-dot-length)

<a id="fig-dot-length" class="ula-anchor"></a>

:::: ula-figure

![A two dimensional vector against a coordinate grid and set of axes.](/figures/ch6/pf-dot-length.svg)

**图 6.1.1** · The vector $\vvec=\twovec32$.

::::

We may find the length of this vector using the Pythagorean theorem since the vector forms the hypotenuse of a right triangle having a horizontal leg of length 3 and a vertical leg of length 2. The length of $\vvec$, which we denote as $\len{\vvec}$, is therefore $\len{\vvec} = \sqrt{3^2 +
2^2} = \sqrt{13}$. Now notice that the dot product of $\vvec$ with itself is 

$$
\begin{aligned}
\vvec\cdot\vvec = 3(3) + 2(2) = 13 = \len{\vvec}^2
\end{aligned}
$$

. This is true in general; that is, we have 

$$
\begin{aligned}
\vvec\cdot\vvec = \len{\vvec}^2\text{.}
\end{aligned}
$$

More than that, the dot product of two vectors records information about the angle between them. Consider [图 6.1.2](/chapter-6/6-1#fig-dot-angle).

<a id="fig-dot-angle" class="ula-anchor"></a>

:::: ula-figure

![Two vectors, the angle between them, and their vector difference.](/figures/ch6/pf-dot-angle.svg)

**图 6.1.2** · The dot product $\vvec\cdot\wvec$ measures the angle $\theta$.

::::

To see this, we will apply the Law of Cosines, which says that 

$$
\begin{aligned}
\len{\wvec-\vvec}^2 & = \len{\vvec}^2 + \len{\wvec}^2 -
2\len{\vvec}\len{\wvec}\cos\theta \\
(\wvec-\vvec)\cdot(\wvec-\vvec) & =
\vvec\cdot\vvec +
\wvec\cdot\wvec - 2\len{\vvec}\len{\wvec}\cos\theta \\
\wvec\cdot\wvec + \vvec\cdot\vvec- 2\vvec\cdot\wvec & =
\vvec\cdot\vvec +
\wvec\cdot\wvec - 2\len{\vvec}\len{\wvec}\cos\theta \\
-2\vvec\cdot\wvec & = -2\len{\vvec}\len{\wvec}\cos\theta \\
\vvec\cdot\wvec & = \len{\vvec}\len{\wvec}\cos\theta \\
\end{aligned}
$$

 The upshot of this reasoning is that 

$$
\begin{aligned}
\vvec\cdot\wvec = \len{\vvec}\len{\wvec}\cos\theta\text{.}
\end{aligned}
$$

To summarize:

:::::: note Geometric properties of the dot product

The dot product gives us the following geometric information: 

$$
\begin{aligned}
\vvec\cdot\vvec& = \len{\vvec}^2 \\
\vvec\cdot\wvec& = \len{\vvec}\len{\wvec}\cos\theta \\
\end{aligned}
$$

 where $\theta$ is the angle between $\vvec$ and $\wvec$.

As we move forward, it will be important for us to recognize when vectors are perpendicular to one another. For instance, when vectors $\vvec$ and $\wvec$ are perpendicular, the angle between them $\theta=90^\circ$ and we have 

$$
\begin{aligned}
\vvec\cdot\wvec=\len{\vvec}\len{\wvec}\cos\theta =
\len{\vvec}\len{\wvec}\cos90^\circ = 0\text{.}
\end{aligned}
$$

 Therefore, the dot product between perpendicular vectors must be zero. This leads to the following definition.

<a id="6-1-definition-1" class="ula-anchor"></a>

:::::: definition 定义 6.1.1

We say that vectors $\vvec$ and $\wvec$ are orthogonal if $\vvec\cdot\wvec=0$.

::::::

In practical terms, two perpendicular vectors are orthogonal. However, the concept of orthogonality is somewhat more general because it allows one or both of the vectors to be the zero vector $\zerovec$.

To see how this could be useful, suppose we have medical data describing a group of patients, some of whom have been diagnosed with a specific condition, such as diabetes. Perhaps we have a record of age, weight, blood sugar, cholesterol, and other attributes for each patient. It could be that the data points for the group diagnosed as having the condition form a cluster that is somewhat distinct from the rest of the data. Suppose that we are able to identify that cluster and that we are then presented with a new patient that has not been tested for the condition. If the attributes for that patient place them in that cluster, we might identify them as being at risk for the condition and prioritize them for appropriate screenings.

If there are many attributes for each patient, the data may be high-dimensional and not easily visualized. We would therefore like to develop an algorithm that separates the data points into clusters without human intervention. We call the result a *clustering*.

The next activity introduces a technique, called $k$-means clustering, that helps us find clusterings. To do so, we will view the data points as vectors so that the distance between two data points equals the length of the vector joining them. That is, if two points are represented by the vectors $\vvec$ and $\wvec$, then the distance between the points is $\len{\vvec-\wvec}$.

This activity demonstrates our algorithm for finding a clustering. We first choose a value $k$ and seek to break the data points into $k$ clusters. The algorithm proceeds in the following way: 

- Choose $k$ points $c_1, c_2, \ldots, c_k$ at random from our dataset.
- Construct the cluster $C_1$ as the set of data points closest to $c_1$, $C_2$ as the set of data points closest to $c_2$, and so forth.
- Repeat the following until the clusters no longer change: 
 
 - Find the centroids $c_1, c_2,\ldots,c_k$ of the current clusters.
 - Update the clusters $C_1,C_2,\ldots,C_k$.

The clusterings we find depend on the initial random choice of points $c_1, c_2,\ldots, c_k$. For instance, in the previous activity, we arrived, with the initial choice $c_1=
\vvec_2$ and $c_2=\vvec_3$, at the clustering: 

$$
\begin{array}{rcl}
C_1 & {}={} & \{\vvec_1\} \\
C_2 & {}={} & \{\vvec_2, \vvec_3,\vvec_4\}.
\end{array}
$$

If we instead choose the initial points to be $c_1 =
\vvec_3$ and $c_2=\vvec_4$, we eventually find the clustering: 

$$
\begin{array}{rcl}
C_1 & {}={} & \{\vvec_1, \vvec_2, \vvec_3\} \\
C_2 & {}={} & \{\vvec_4\}.
\end{array}
$$

Is there a way that we can determine which clustering is the better of the two? It seems like a better clustering will be one for which the points in a cluster are, on average, closer to the centroid of their cluster. If we have a clustering, we therefore define a function, called the *objective*, which measures the average of the square of the distance from each point to the centroid of the cluster to which that point belongs. A clustering with a smaller objective will have clusters more tightly centered around their centroids, which should result in a better clustering.

For example, when we obtain the clustering: 

$$
\begin{array}{rcl}
C_1 & {}={} & \{\vvec_1, \vvec_2, \vvec_3\} \\
C_2 & {}={} & \{\vvec_4\}.
\end{array}
$$

 with centroids $c_1=\ctwovec{0}{4/3}$ and $c_2=\vvec_4=\twovec32$, we find the objective to be 

$$
\begin{aligned}
\frac14\left(
\len{\vvec_1-c_1}^2 +
\len{\vvec_2-c_1}^2 +
\len{\vvec_3-c_1}^2 +
\len{\vvec_4-c_2}^2
\right)
= \frac 53.
\end{aligned}
$$

This section introduced the dot product and the ability to investigate geometric relationships between vectors. 

- The dot product of two vectors $\vvec$ and $\wvec$ satisfies these properties: 
 
 $$
 \begin{array}{rcl}
 \vvec\cdot\vvec & {}={} & \len{\vvec}^2 \\
 \vvec\cdot\wvec & {}={} &
 \len{\vvec}\len{\wvec}\cos\theta \\
 \end{array}
 $$
 
 where $\theta$ is the angle between $\vvec$ and $\wvec$.
- The vectors $\vvec$ and $\wvec$ are orthogonal when $\vvec\cdot\wvec= 0$.
- We explored some applications of the dot product to the similarity of vectors, correlation of time series, and $k$-means clustering.

:::::: exercise 练习 6.1.5 · Proving the relationship between dot product and magnitude

Suppose $\vvec=\twovec{x_1}{x_2}$. Prove $\vvec\cdot\vvec=\len{\vvec}^2$.

$$
\begin{aligned}
\vvec\cdot\vvec = x_1^2+x_2^2.
\end{aligned}
$$

$$
\begin{aligned}
\vvec\cdot\vvec = \left(\sqrt{x_1^2+x_2^2}\right)^2.
\end{aligned}
$$

$$
\begin{aligned}
\vvec\cdot\vvec = \len{\vvec}^2.
\end{aligned}
$$

$$
\begin{aligned}
\sqrt{\vvec\cdot\vvec} = \len{\vvec}.
\end{aligned}
$$
